CAP Theorem & Consistency Models

Every distributed design is haunted by one question: when the network splits, do you keep answering, or do you stay correct? CAP names that trade-off, and consistency models describe exactly how correct you promise to be. Once you can place any system on this map, half of every "design X" discussion writes itself.

01

What is CAP?

The CAP theorem, published by Eric Brewer in 2000, says: a distributed data store can only guarantee two of three properties at the same time — Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance. Interviewers use it as a lens: "tell me how your design behaves when the network between your servers fails."

Distributed system

A system whose data or logic runs on more than one machine (called nodes), and where those machines talk to each other over a network. The moment you add a replica of your database, a second app server, or a cache in another region, you have one. The price of having more than one machine is that the network between them can break.

The three letters, from zero

C — Consistency

Every read sees the most recent write — no matter which node you ask. If a goal is recorded on one server, every other server's answer must include that goal, instantly. It is not about your database being tidy; it is specifically about all copies agreeing on the latest value.

A — Availability

Every request gets a non-error answer. A node must reply — it may not hang, time out, or refuse. Note what this does not promise: the answer can be stale or wrong, and that still counts as "available." Availability is about responsiveness, not correctness.

P — Partition tolerance

The system keeps working even when the network splits and nodes can't reach each other — a partition. Partitions happen for boring reasons: a cut fiber cable, a misconfigured firewall, a switch rebooting, a cloud region losing connectivity. You don't get to vote on whether partitions happen; the only question is how your system behaves during one.

⚽ The running example: the World Cup final scoreboard

Two stadiums, one score

Picture the World Cup final. The official score is kept on a server at Stadium East. A second copy runs at Stadium West across town, feeding the giant screens and the club app. The two copies sync over a fiber link. Tens of thousands of fans in each stadium stare at screens and ask, constantly: "What's the score?"

In the 63rd minute, a striker scores. The write lands at Stadium East: 1–0. Now, before the update can cross the fiber link, a construction crew cuts the cable. The network is partitioned. A fan in the West stadium looks up at the screen and the app asks the West server for the score. What should it say?

This question — 0–0, or "please wait"? — is the entire CAP theorem. Keep this picture in your head; every idea in this lesson lands on it.

02

The real trade-off: partitions are not optional

The textbook version says "pick two of three." The working version is sharper: in a distributed system, P is not a choice — so the real choice is between C and A during a partition. Once you internalize that, the theorem stops being trivia and starts being a design tool.

Why "pick 2 of 3" is misleading

Textbooks present CAP as a menu: choose C+P, A+P, or C+A. But "C+A" means "give up partition tolerance" — i.e., pretend the network never fails. A single machine with no replicas is C+A, and that's fine. The instant you have two machines holding copies of data, partitions become physically possible, and you must have a plan for them. So a distributed system never really chooses between three letters; it chooses:

  • During a partition: consistency or availability — never both.
  • Outside a partition (network healthy, which is 99.9%+ of the time): you tune latency vs. consistency — a different trade-off entirely (see PACELC, section 6).

Walk through the partition, step by step

Back to our stadiums. Fiber is cut. Score is 1–0 at East, still 0–0 at West. A West fan asks for the score. Two honest strategies:

Strategy 1: stay consistent (CP — Consistency + Partition tolerance)

The West screen refuses to answer: "Signal lost — verifying score." The fan gets no answer — that's unavailability. But nobody in the West stadium sees a wrong score. The system chose C over A.

The price: if the fiber stays cut for an hour, the West stadium has no scoreboard at all for an hour. Fans riot. Consistency is not free.

Strategy 2: stay available (AP — Availability + Partition tolerance)

The West screen keeps answering: "0–0". Every fan in the West stadium sees the wrong score — but the screens work, the app responds, nobody stares at a dead display. The system chose A over C.

The price: thousands of fans believe it's 0–0 when it's 1–0. When the fiber is repaired, West catches up and the screens flip to 1–0. Fans saw stale data for an hour. Availability is not free either.

The interviewer trap: "So which is better, CP or AP?" There is no universal answer — the correct answer is always "it depends on the workload." Selling the last concert ticket twice (double-selling one seat) is catastrophic → lean CP. Showing a slightly stale like-count is harmless → lean AP. Saying "it depends" with a concrete example of each is what separates a senior answer from a memorized one.

The consistency–availability choice is per-operation, not per-system

Real systems don't pick one letter and wear it forever. They pick per feature:

  • A concert-ticket platform is CP for seat inventory (never sell seat 14B twice) and AP for the "trending shows" feed (a stale trending list hurts nobody).
  • A bank is CP for balances (your money must be exactly right) and AP for the marketing banner on the login page.

Saying "my system is CP" without naming which operations is a junior answer. The senior version: "We're CP on the write path for inventory, AP on reads for the catalog."

04

Consistency models: how correct, exactly?

CAP's "C" is a light switch — everyone agrees, instantly. Real systems need dimmer settings: consistency models are the formal promises a system makes about what readers can see and when. They run from "everyone sees everything instantly" down to "you'll see it… eventually." Each model below is defined from zero, with the same soccer scenario so you can compare them directly.

⚽ The shared scenario

The club app's transfer-news feed

Your club's app shows transfer news, fed by two writers: the club reporter (posts official news) and a fan blogger (posts reactions). Two fans, Ada and Ben, read the feed from different phones hitting different replicas. Three posts happen in order: P1: "Star striker injured in training" → P2: "Club signs replacement striker" → P3: "Replacement scores on debut".

Watch what Ada and Ben are allowed to see under each model.

The spectrum, strongest first

1. Strong consistency (linearizability)

Definition: every read sees the latest completed write, as if there were only one copy and all operations happened in a single global order. Once a write is acknowledged, every subsequent read anywhere returns it.

Soccer: the moment the reporter hits "publish" on P2, Ada's and Ben's phones both show P2 — no exceptions, no delay you can observe.

Cost: the most expensive model. Every write must be confirmed by enough replicas before it returns, which adds latency — and during a partition, writes must block (CP), because a replica can't be sure it's the latest. Used for money, inventory, locks, configuration.

2. Sequential consistency

Definition: all nodes see operations in the same order, and that order respects each writer's own program order — but the shared order doesn't have to match real time. Everyone agrees on the sequence of events; they just might all agree on a sequence that is a few seconds behind reality.

Soccer: Ada and Ben both see P1, then P2, then P3 — never P2 before P1, and never a different order from each other. But both phones might still be showing P1 for a moment after P2 was published.

Cost: slightly cheaper than strong (no real-time guarantee), but still requires global coordination. Rarely named as a product feature; mostly a textbook step on the ladder.

3. Causal consistency

Definition: operations that are causally related — one could have influenced the other — appear in order everywhere. Operations with no causal link may appear in any order. "Causally related" usually means: same writer, or a read-then-write chain (you saw X, then wrote Y because of X).

Soccer: P2 ("signs replacement") was written because of P1 ("striker injured") — they're causally linked, so nobody ever sees P2 without P1. But the fan blogger's reaction post (unrelated to the reporter's news) can appear before or after P1 on different phones.

Cost: much cheaper — replicas only need to track cause-and-effect chains (often via vector clocks, timestamps that record "who knew what when"), not a global order. A sweet spot for collaborative apps and social feeds.

4. Session guarantees: read-your-writes & monotonic reads

Definition: these promise consistency within one user's session, not across the whole system. Read-your-writes: after you write something, your own subsequent reads always show it. Monotonic reads: once you've seen a value, you never see an older value again — time never runs backward for you.

Soccer: the reporter publishes P2, then refreshes her own phone: she must see P2 (read-your-writes) — otherwise she'd think the publish failed and post it twice. Meanwhile Ben's phone might lag behind, as long as it never shows him P1 after already showing him P2 (monotonic reads).

Cost: cheap to provide — usually done by sticky routing (sending the same user to the same replica) or by having clients remember a version token. This is why your own post always appears on your screen even when everyone else's feed is stale.

5. Eventual consistency

Definition: the weakest promise — if writes stop, all replicas will eventually agree. There is no bound on "eventually": it could be milliseconds or minutes. Readers may see old values, see updates in different orders, and temporarily disagree with each other.

Soccer: after P3 is published, Ada might see P3 while Ben still sees P1 — and if Ben refreshes, he could briefly see P1 again (no monotonic-read guarantee either, unless added separately). Once the newsroom goes quiet, both phones converge to P1→P2→P3.

Cost: the cheapest and most available model — writes succeed with a single replica, so the system stays up through partitions (AP). Used for like counts, view counts, product catalogs, DNS, and social feeds. The catch: your application must tolerate temporary disagreement.

Compare them at a glance

ModelGuarantee in one lineWhat a reader might seeTypical use
StrongEveryone sees the latest write, instantlyAlways the truth, never staleBank balances, seat inventory, locks
SequentialEveryone sees the same orderAgreed order, possibly behind real timeTextbook model; total-order broadcast
CausalCause-and-effect order preservedRelated posts ordered; unrelated may shuffleSocial feeds, collaborative docs

Common confusion: "eventual consistency" ≠ "no consistency." It is a precise, testable guarantee: convergence. What it does not guarantee is when. And a separate question: when two replicas accept conflicting writes during a partition (both stadiums record a goal at the same time), conflict resolution decides the winner — last-write-wins (by timestamp), application merge (the app reconciles), or CRDTs (data structures mathematically designed to merge without conflicts). You covered these in the replication lesson; they are the machinery that makes "eventually" actually happen.

06

PACELC: what happens when nothing breaks

Here's CAP's blind spot: partitions are rare. Your system spends 99.9% of its life not partitioned — and CAP says nothing about that time. PACELC (by Daniel Abadi) completes the picture.

PACELC

If there is a Partition, choose between Availability and Consistency (that's CAP). Else (no partition — the normal case), choose between Latency and Consistency.

Read that second half carefully, because it's where senior engineers spend their time. Even with a perfectly healthy network, you face a choice on every write:

  • Wait for replicas to confirm (synchronous replication) → stronger consistency, higher latency. The fan's "publish" button spins a little longer, but once it succeeds, every replica has it.
  • Return as soon as one replica has it (asynchronous replication) → lower latency, weaker consistency. The button responds instantly, but a reader on another replica might not see the post yet.

Back to the stadiums: with the fiber healthy, the club app team still decides — does the reporter's "publish" wait for the West stadium to confirm (EL: latency, EC: consistency)? Match-day traffic spikes might push them toward async (low latency), while the official match record might demand sync (consistency). Same system, two different ELC choices for two different features — exactly like the per-operation CAP choices in section 2.

Why interviewers love PACELC

Most candidates recite "pick two of three" and stop. Saying "and under PACELC, when there's no partition we trade latency for consistency — so for the write path I'd use synchronous replication because seat inventory can't tolerate stale reads, accepting the extra ~20ms" signals you think about the system as it runs 99.9% of the time, not just during disasters. That's a senior-level sentence.

Where real systems sit on the map

Theory is forgettable; placements are memorable. Here is where well-known systems land — and why, because the "why" is what interviewers probe.

SystemCAP sideWhy it chose that side
ZooKeeper / etcdCPThey store coordination data — locks, leader election, config. A wrong answer here corrupts everything built on top, so they'd rather refuse than lie. During a partition the minority side stops serving.
Cassandra / DynamoDBAPBuilt for shopping carts and always-on writes. A partition must not stop checkouts; conflicting writes are merged later (timestamps / vector clocks). You can tune it toward C with quorum settings, but its soul is availability.
MongoDBCP (default), tunableBy default, writes go to the primary and the primary steps down if it can't reach a majority — choosing consistency. But you can relax read/write concerns per operation, sliding toward AP.
PostgreSQL (single node)CAOne machine, no partition possible inside it: fully consistent and fully available. The moment you add replicas or failover, you're back in distributed land and CA evaporates.
Google SpannerCP, with a twistUses atomic clocks (TrueTime) to give external consistency — strong consistency with real-time ordering — across the planet. It chose consistency and paid for it with extraordinary infrastructure.
Redis ClusterAP (default)Async replication by default: fast, available, but a failover can lose acknowledged writes. Tunable toward CP with WAIT, but most deployments accept the risk for speed.
KafkaCP (typical config)With acks=all and min.insync.replicas, a partition blocks producers rather than risk losing or duplicating committed messages. Relax those settings and it slides toward AP.

The "tunable" trap. Notice how many rows say tunable. Modern systems are rarely pure CP or AP — they expose knobs (quorum sizes, write concerns, ack levels) that slide along the spectrum per operation. If an interviewer asks "is Cassandra CP or AP?", the strong answer is: "AP by default and by design philosophy, but with R + W > N quorum reads/writes you can get strong consistency per operation — you just pay latency and lose availability during partitions for those operations." That sentence demonstrates the replication lesson, the CAP lesson, and the per-operation thinking all at once.

08

How to use CAP in a design discussion

CAP is not a slide you present — it's a decision you announce, per component, with a reason. Here is the three-sentence pattern that works in any design interview:

The pattern: name it, place it, justify it

  1. Name the data: "For seat inventory…", "For the trending feed…"
  2. Place it: "…I'll go CP / AP / strong / eventual…"
  3. Justify it with the cost of being wrong: "…because double-selling a seat is a refund and a lawsuit, while a stale trending list is invisible."

Then add the PACELC kicker for the normal case: "With no partition, I'll use synchronous replication on the inventory write path — I'll pay ~20ms of latency so reads never see a sold seat as available."

A worked example: concert ticketing

  • Seat inventory (CP, strong): two buyers must never hold the same seat. During a partition, the minority region stops selling rather than risk a double-sale. Users see "try again" — annoying, but the business survives.
  • Order history (session consistency): after you buy, your "my tickets" page must show the ticket immediately (read-your-writes) — otherwise you'll buy twice. Nobody else needs to see it instantly.
  • Trending shows feed (AP, eventual): a concert showing 9,999 vs 10,000 tickets sold changes nothing. Serve it from the nearest replica, reconcile later.

Three components, three different answers, each with a one-line justification. That is what "knowing CAP" looks like in practice — not the triangle diagram, but the discipline of assigning consistency per feature based on the cost of being wrong.

09

Interview Q&A — with full model answers

Tap a question to reveal a complete model answer — the kind of answer that earns the follow-up question instead of a blank stare. Each one is written to be spoken aloud in about a minute.

Q1 · THE 60-SECOND ANSWER
Explain the CAP theorem.
The opener. Almost every distributed-systems interview starts here — they want the crisp version, then they'll probe.
Reveal model answer

"CAP says a distributed data store can only guarantee two of three things at the same time: Consistency — every read sees the latest write; Availability — every request gets a non-error response; and Partition tolerance — the system keeps working when the network splits nodes apart.

The practical reading is sharper than 'pick two of three.' In a distributed system, partitions will happen — cables get cut, switches reboot — so P isn't really a choice. The real choice is what you do during a partition: stay consistent by refusing requests you can't verify, or stay available by answering with possibly stale data.

And per PACELC, there's a second trade-off when the network is healthy: latency vs. consistency on every write. So in a design I'd choose per component — CP with strong consistency for things like seat inventory where being wrong is expensive, AP with eventual consistency for things like like-counts where staleness is harmless."

Q2 · APPLIED JUDGMENT
You're designing a concert ticketing system. CP or AP — and where?
They're testing whether you apply CAP per component instead of blessing the whole system with one letter.
Reveal model answer

"It splits by component, based on the cost of being wrong:

  • Seat inventory — CP, strong consistency. Double-selling seat 14B means refunds, angry customers, possibly legal trouble. During a partition, the minority side stops selling rather than risk it. I'd use synchronous replication on the write path — pay ~20ms latency so no read ever sees a sold seat as available.
  • Order history — session consistency. After buying, your 'my tickets' page must show the ticket immediately — read-your-writes — or you'll panic and buy twice. Other users don't need to see it instantly.
  • Trending feed and seat maps — AP, eventual consistency. A concert showing 9,999 vs 10,000 tickets sold changes no decision. Serve from the nearest replica, reconcile in the background.

So the honest answer isn't 'CP or AP' — it's CP where correctness has a price tag, AP where staleness is invisible, and I'd state that split explicitly in the design."

Q3 · THE TRAP QUESTION
Can a system be CA — consistent and available, giving up partition tolerance?
Classic trap. They want to see if you understand that P is not optional in distributed systems.
Reveal model answer

"Only if it's not really distributed. A single-node PostgreSQL is CA — one machine, no network between copies, so partitions can't happen inside it. It's consistent and available.

But the moment you add a replica, a failover standby, or a second region — the moment it's distributed — partitions become physically possible, and 'giving up P' just means 'having no plan for when the network fails,' which is not a plan. So CA exists, but only on a single box, and single boxes don't survive hardware failure — which is why we distribute in the first place.

The follow-up I'd add unprompted: PACELC covers the 99.9% of time when there's no partition, where the real trade-off is latency vs. consistency on the write path."

Q4 · PRECISION
What does eventual consistency actually guarantee? Give a concrete example where it's the right choice — and one where it would be dangerous.
They're checking you know "eventual" is a real guarantee (convergence), not a shrug.
Reveal model answer

"Eventual consistency guarantees convergence: if writes stop, all replicas will eventually agree on the same state. What it does not guarantee is when — it could be milliseconds or minutes — or what any individual read returns in the meantime. Readers may see stale, reordered, or conflicting values.

Right choice: a video's view count on a streaming platform. If two viewers see 1.2M vs 1.21M for a minute, nobody is harmed, and the system stays fast and available through anything. The counter converges.

Dangerous: a bank transfer ledger. If your balance reads $500 on one replica and $300 on another because a transfer hasn't converged, you might spend money twice — or be wrongly declined. Money needs strong consistency; the cost of being wrong is the money itself.

One more nuance I'd mention: eventual systems still need conflict resolution for writes that land on both sides of a partition — last-write-wins by timestamp, application-level merge, or CRDTs — because 'converge' has to mean converge to the same thing."

Practice drills

Untimed — think like you're whiteboarding. Write your answer, then check the rubric.

Drill 1 — Split the food-delivery app

A food-delivery app has four features: (a) restaurant menu browsing, (b) placing an order (kitchen must not get two copies of the same order), (c) the courier's live location on your map, (d) your order history. Assign each a CAP side and consistency model, with a one-line justification each.

Reveal rubric
  • (a) Menus — AP, eventual. A menu showing yesterday's price for a minute is harmless; browsing must never go down during a partition. (Full marks for "stale menu is invisible to the business.")
  • (b) Order placement — CP, strong. A duplicated order means the kitchen cooks twice and the customer is charged twice — the cost of being wrong is real money. During a partition, refuse new orders on the minority side. (Full marks for naming the double-charge failure.)
  • (c) Courier location — AP, eventual (or even weaker). Location data is stale by nature — it's always a few seconds old. Availability matters; precision doesn't. (Full marks for "location is inherently stale anyway.")
  • (d) Order history — session consistency. You must see your just-placed order immediately (read-your-writes, or you'll reorder); nobody else cares. (Full marks for the per-user framing.)

What earns top marks: the phrase "the cost of being wrong" applied per feature, plus explicitly stating what happens during a partition for the CP choice (refuse orders) rather than hand-waving.

Drill 2 — Classify and defend

For each system, state its CAP side and one sentence of why: etcd, Cassandra, single-node PostgreSQL, Redis Cluster (default config).

Reveal rubric
  • etcd — CP. It stores coordination data (locks, leader election); a wrong answer corrupts everything above it, so the minority partition stops serving.
  • Cassandra — AP. Designed for always-on writes (shopping carts); conflicts merge later via timestamps. Tunable toward C per operation with quorums.
  • Single-node PostgreSQL — CA. One box, no internal partition possible — consistent and available until the hardware dies. Add a replica and CA is gone.
  • Redis Cluster default — AP. Asynchronous replication: fast and available, but a failover can lose acknowledged writes. WAIT can slide it toward CP.

What earns top marks: adding "tunable" where it applies (Cassandra quorums, Redis WAIT) instead of treating the labels as permanent tattoos.

Drill 3 — The PACELC sentence

In one or two sentences, explain the trade-off your system makes when there is no partition — for the write path of seat inventory in a ticketing system. Mention latency explicitly.

Reveal rubric

A strong answer sounds like: "With no partition, I use synchronous replication on the inventory write path — the buyer's request waits for a quorum of replicas to confirm, costing roughly tens of milliseconds of latency, so that no read anywhere can ever see a sold seat as available."

What earns top marks: naming the mechanism (sync replication / quorum), naming the price (latency, quantified even roughly), and tying it to the user-visible guarantee. Mentioning "PACELC" by name is a bonus, not a requirement.

✓

Key takeaways

  • CAP: during a partition, a distributed store chooses between consistency (refuse rather than lie) and availability (answer, possibly stale). Partitions aren't optional — so the choice isn't either.
  • "Pick two of three" is the slogan; per-operation trade-offs are the job. Real systems are CP for inventory and AP for feeds, in the same codebase.
  • Consistency models are the dimmer switch: strong → sequential → causal → session → eventual. Each step down buys performance or availability and sells certainty.
  • Eventual consistency guarantees convergence — not speed. And it needs conflict resolution to converge to the same thing.
  • PACELC covers the 99.9% healthy-network case: latency vs. consistency on the write path. This is where senior engineers actually spend their design time.
  • In interviews: name the data, place it on the map, justify it with the cost of being wrong — one sentence per component.
