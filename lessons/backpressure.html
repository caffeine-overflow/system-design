Lesson 23 · Advanced Trade-off

Backpressure — When Demand Outruns Capacity

Every system you will ever design has a ceiling: the number of requests per second it can actually finish. Most days you never touch it. But the day 500,000 fans show up for 40,000 seats, or a retry bug multiplies your traffic five-fold overnight, the ceiling is the whole design. Backpressure is the art of surviving that day: deciding, before the surge, what the system does when more work arrives than it can finish.

01

What is backpressure?

The whole lesson in one scene: 10:00 AM, Riverside FC opens the derby ticket presale. 500,000 fans hit the site at once for 40,000 seats. The checkout pipeline can process 300 checkouts a second. In the first 60 seconds, 500,000 requests arrive and 18,000 finish. What happens to the other 482,000 — and what should happen — is backpressure.

Backpressure

Any mechanism by which an overloaded component slows down the work flowing into it. The name is literal: when a pipe is too narrow for the water, pressure builds up — and instead of letting the pipe burst, you push pressure backwards toward the source, asking it to ease off. Backpressure is the feedback signal that travels from the bottleneck upstream to the sender: "I can't take this much. Slow down."

The vocabulary, from zero

  • Arrival rate (λ) — how fast work arrives, in requests per second. Lambda is the demand side. In the presale: roughly 500,000 fans in 60 seconds ≈ 8,333 requests/sec in the first minute.
  • Service rate (μ) — how fast the system can finish work, in requests per second. Mu is the capacity side. The checkout pipeline: 300 checkouts/sec.
  • Queue depth — how much work is currently waiting. If λ > μ, the queue grows at roughly (λ − μ) per second. After 60 seconds of presale: (8,333 − 300) × 60 ≈ 482,000 waiting.
  • Utilization (ρ = λ/μ) — how busy the system is. ρ = 1 means exactly saturated. ρ = 27.8 in the presale minute means the system is nearly 28× over capacity. Backpressure is the regime where ρ > 1, i.e. λ > μ.
  • Saturation — the state of running at full capacity. A saturated system has zero spare capacity: every new arrival either waits or is refused. Saturation is normal in a well-used system; it only becomes an incident when it goes unmanaged.
  • Load shedding — deliberately refusing or dropping the lowest-value work so the rest survives. The bouncer who turns away the rowdiest arrivals so the venue stays safe.
  • Flow control — the sender and receiver agreeing on the pace: the receiver tells the sender "send me only this much". TCP does this; so do modern streaming libraries.

⚽ The soccer picture to keep

Picture the stadium gates at a sold-out derby. Fans (requests) pour down the street toward the turnstiles (your servers). When the concourse inside is full, the stewards don't let people pile into the stairwells — they stop the flow at the outer gate and tell the crowd to wait in the square outside. That signal — from the full concourse, back out to the street — is backpressure. Notice three things: (1) the signal flows backwards, from the bottleneck to the source; (2) the crowd in the square is a cheap place to wait (no pressure on the turnstiles); (3) the stewards also decide who gets in first (season-ticket holders' lane) — that's load shedding and priorities. Every mechanism in this lesson is one of those three steward moves.

Why this lesson exists

Junior answers to "design a ticket sale" describe the happy path: checkout flow, payment, seat map, confirmation email. Senior answers start at ρ = 27.8 and design the unhappy path first, because the unhappy path is the entire problem. Two facts make backpressure non-optional:

  • You can't make μ infinite. Databases don't autoscale writes, payment providers rate-limit you, and every new server takes minutes to boot — the surge arrives in seconds.
  • Doing nothing is a decision with the worst outcomes. An unmanaged queue grows unboundedly: memory runs out, latencies hit minutes, timeouts fire, clients retry — and every retry is new demand on an already-saturated system. Doing nothing doesn't absorb the surge; it converts the surge into a cascade.

A note on scope: Lesson 21 used the season-ticket checkout to discuss getting all-or-nothing right across databases. Different match, different problem. That lesson was about correctness when the work is modest. This one is about survival when the work is enormous.

02

The math of overload: arrival rate vs service rate

Backpressure is one of the rare design topics where three tiny formulas answer most questions. Everything in this section is worked with the derby presale numbers so the math sticks to a story.

Formula 1: the queue grows at (λ − μ) per second

This is almost insultingly simple, which is why it's powerful. If 8,333 fans arrive per second and 300 finish per second, the queue gains 8,033 per second:

λ = 8,333 req/s (first minute)    μ = 300 req/s
queue growth = λ − μ = 8,033 req/s
after 60 s: 8,033 × 60 ≈ 482,000 waiting

The design insight: a queue is a bet that λ > μ is temporary. If the surge lasts 60 seconds and your queue can hold 482,000 requests' worth of state, fine. If the surge lasts an hour — or forever, because timeouts turned one request into five (section 05) — the bet loses. Every queue decision is really a decision about how long you're willing to bet the imbalance lasts.

Formula 2: Little's Law — queue × wait are two views of one thing

Little's Law says: the average number of items in a system L equals the arrival rate λ times the average time each item spends in the system W:

L = λ × W        →        W = L ÷ λ

It holds for any system — a checkout pipeline, a stadium queue, a hospital ER. Applied to the presale: a fan arriving to find 482,000 people ahead of her, with the pipeline finishing 300 per second, waits:

W = 482,000 ÷ 300 ≈ 1,607 seconds ≈ 27 minutes

That number is the one that matters to the business: 27 minutes of waiting is when fans refresh the page, open five tabs, and call customer support — each of which is new demand. Little's Law is how you convert "the queue is big" into "the user experience is broken", which is how you justify the backpressure design to anyone who isn't an engineer.

Formula 3: latency explodes as utilization approaches 100%

For a single server with random arrivals, the average wait grows as 1 / (μ − λ). Watch what happens as λ creeps toward μ:

μ = 300/s.   λ = 150/s (ρ = 0.5)  → wait ≈ 1/(300−150)  ≈ 6.7 ms
             λ = 270/s (ρ = 0.9)  → wait ≈ 1/(300−270)  ≈ 33 ms
             λ = 295/s (ρ = 0.98) → wait ≈ 1/(300−295)   ≈ 200 ms
             λ = 300/s (ρ = 1.0)  → wait → unbounded

The curve has a knee: from ρ = 0.5 to ρ = 0.9, wait grows 5×; from 0.9 to 0.98, another 6× — and past 1.0 there is no steady state at all, just a queue that grows forever. Two consequences experienced designers never forget:

  • Capacity headroom is a latency feature, not waste. Running at 60–70% utilization isn't laziness — it's buying the flat part of the curve. This is why autoscaling targets are usually set at ~70%, not ~100%.
  • A tiny capacity loss at high utilization is a huge latency event. If one database replica dies and μ drops 10% while λ stays put, you can jump from ρ = 0.9 to ρ = 1.0 instantly — the incident isn't "we lost 10% of capacity", it's "the queue started growing forever".

The one-line math verdict (say it first in the room): "The presale arrives at 8,333 a second against 300 a second of checkout capacity — ρ ≈ 28. The queue grows at 8,000 a second, and by Little's Law the last fan waits 27 minutes. No happy-path design survives that; the question is what the system does with the 482,000 it can't serve."

03

The five responses: buffer, scale, shed, push back, degrade

When λ > μ, there are exactly five things a system can do. They differ in what they buy you, what they cost, and how fast they take effect — which is why real designs combine two or three of them. Work through each with the presale running.

1. Buffer — queue the overflow

Hold the extra work and serve it when capacity frees up. This is the default instinct, and it's the right first lever for short bursts: a queue absorbs a 30-second spike beautifully.

But a queue is not free. Three prices:

  • Latency. Every queued request waits (Little's Law). Buffering 482,000 checkouts means 27-minute waits — technically "no one is dropped", practically everyone is angry.
  • Memory. A queue holds connections and state, and those are expensive. 482,000 open HTTP connections at ~100 KB of kernel buffers each ≈ 48 GB of RAM — plus your servers' thread pools, which typically cap at a few hundred threads each. The queue dies of memory long before it drains.
  • State decay. Queued work goes stale: a held checkout whose payment authorization expires, a held seat that the fan no longer wants. The longer the buffer, the more of it is waste.

The senior rule: buffering is for absorbing seconds of burst, not minutes of imbalance. Size the queue for the burst you expect, bound it hard, and have a plan for when it fills — which is the next three levers.

Bounded queue

A queue with a hard maximum size. When full, new arrivals are refused (see shedding) instead of accepted. An unbounded queue accepts everything until the process runs out of memory — which is how "just queue it" becomes an out-of-memory crash at 2 AM. In every design you'll ever draw, the box labeled "queue" gets a number next to it: its bound.

2. Scale — add capacity

Raise μ to meet λ: boot more checkout servers, add workers. The only lever that serves everyone — and the slowest one to take effect:

  • It takes minutes; the surge takes seconds. A new VM or container needs 2–5 minutes to boot, warm its caches, and join the load balancer. The presale crush peaks in the first 60.
  • Not everything scales. Stateless checkout frontends scale fine. The database writes underneath them don't autoscale — and neither does your payment provider, which will rate-limit you no matter how many servers you boot. Scaling moves the bottleneck; it rarely removes it.
  • It costs money for the 364 days you don't need it. Hence autoscaling (automatically adding/removing servers based on a metric like CPU or queue depth), usually targeting ~70% utilization for the latency reasons in section 02.

Scale is a planned lever — you design the system to be scalable (stateless, partitionable) so that when the surge comes, the lever exists. It is not a reaction you improvise mid-surge. ⚽ The stewards can't build a bigger concourse during the match — but the club can open the third gate that was designed into the stadium.

3. Shed load — refuse the lowest-value work

When the queue is full, stop accepting. Return an error fast — 503 Service Unavailable — so the client knows immediately instead of waiting 27 minutes to find out.

Shedding is where priorities enter the design. Not all requests are equal, and a good shedding policy says so explicitly:

  • Shed bots and scrapers first. Rate-limit aggressive IPs and user agents (see Lesson 11) — presales attract scalper bots, and dropping them is pure win.
  • Shed cheap work last? Actually the reverse: shed cheap-to-retry, low-value work first. The fan browsing the seat map can be told "come back in 10 minutes" — the fan mid-checkout with a payment hold cannot be dropped without losing the sale.
  • Never shed the money path silently. A shed checkout must fail loudly and cleanly: release the seat hold, tell the fan exactly what happened ("the presale is at capacity — your place in the queue is #412,318"), and give a retry time. A silent drop is how you double-charge people.

The HTTP contract for shedding: 503 with a Retry-After header (a number of seconds the client should wait before trying again). Retry-After is the polite form of backpressure — the server telling the client exactly how long to back off, instead of the client guessing. ⚽ The steward doesn't just turn fans away — he tells them "come back to this gate in 20 minutes", which spreads the crowd instead of compressing it.

4. Push back — tell the sender to slow down

Instead of refusing work at the door, send a signal upstream: ease off. The difference from shedding is direction: shedding is the server protecting itself; push-back is the server coordinating with its callers.

  • 429 Too Many Requests — rate-limiting's way of pushing back (Lesson 11). Per-client limits turn one fan's 50 refreshes into 1 effective request.
  • TCP flow control — the receiver advertises a receive window ("I have room for N more bytes"), and the sender is forbidden from exceeding it. The original backpressure, baked into the internet since the 1980s.
  • Reactive streams' request(n) — the consumer says "send me exactly n items", and the producer sends only n. Demand flows upstream before data flows downstream. This is the "pull" model of backpressure: the slow side sets the pace, by design.
  • Kafka consumer pause — a consumer falling behind pauses fetching from a partition; lag grows in the broker (cheap disk) instead of in the consumer (expensive memory).

The catch: push-back only works if the sender cooperates. TCP senders must obey the window; reactive producers must honor request(n). A browser user hammering refresh doesn't cooperate — which is why public-facing systems pair push-back (for well-behaved clients) with shedding (for the rest). ⚽ The stewards can ask the crowd to wait — but the fans who vault the barrier get ejected. Cooperation and enforcement, together.

5. Degrade — serve a cheaper answer

Lower μ's cost per request so the same capacity serves more of them. Degradation trades quality for throughput:

  • Serve cached/static content. The seat map becomes a static image instead of a live interactive query — 10× cheaper per request.
  • Turn off expensive features. Recommendations, personalization, real-time seat-availability counts — off during the surge, back after. A simplified checkout (fewer steps, no upsells) also raises μ directly.
  • Batch and coalesce. Ten fans asking for the same seat-map tile get one computed answer (see Lesson 3's stampede mitigations).

Degradation needs feature flags (switches you can flip without deploying) and a pre-decided degradation order: which features go first, second, third. Deciding the order mid-incident is how you accidentally turn off checkout instead of recommendations.

Same five moves, whether the bottleneck is a turnstile or a checkout pipeline.

04

Backpressure in real systems

The five levers aren't theory — they're the same handful of mechanisms wearing different uniforms. Recognize the uniform and you can read any system's backpressure story in minutes.

TCP: the original backpressure

Every TCP connection runs flow control. The receiver advertises a receive window — "I currently have room for N bytes" — in every acknowledgment, and the sender may not send more than the window allows. Fast sender, slow receiver? The window shrinks to zero and the sender stops — no buffering explosion, no data loss, just waiting. Then the window reopens and flow resumes. This is push-back in its purest form: the slow side sets the pace, and the protocol enforces it. Everything below is a re-invention of this idea for higher layers.

Reactive streams: request(n)

Libraries like RxJava, Project Reactor, and Akka Streams formalize the pull model: a subscriber sends request(n) — "I'm ready for n items" — and the publisher is forbidden from sending n+1. Demand flows upstream; data flows downstream only to fill expressed demand. Why it matters for your designs: when a service consumes from a fast producer (a Kafka topic, an event firehose), the reactive pattern means the consumer can never be overwhelmed — it simply requests more when it's ready. The queue lives in the producer's buffer, which is the cheap, designed-for-it place.

Kafka: let lag live in the broker

A Kafka consumer that falls behind doesn't buffer millions of messages in its own memory — it pauses fetching and lets the backlog sit in the broker's partitions (cheap disk, designed for retention). Consumer lag (how far behind the latest offset a consumer is) becomes the backpressure gauge: alert on lag growth rate, not just lag, because a stable lag of 1M messages is fine and a lag growing at 10K/sec is an incident in progress. This is the "cheap place to wait" principle from the stadium: the broker is the square outside the gates.

HTTP APIs: 429, 503, and Retry-After

The web's backpressure vocabulary is three status codes plus one header:

  • 429 Too Many Requests — "you specifically are sending too much" (per-client push-back; Lesson 11).
  • 503 Service Unavailable — "I'm overloaded for everyone right now" (shedding).
  • Retry-After: 30 — "try again in 30 seconds" — the coordination that turns shedding from a rejection into a schedule.

The header is the difference between a stampede and an orderly queue: without it, every shed client retries immediately; with it, retries spread out. Always pair a 429/503 with Retry-After. Always.

Adaptive concurrency limits

Fixed limits ("max 200 concurrent requests") are guesses; traffic patterns change. Adaptive concurrency limits (Netflix's concurrency-limits library is the canonical example) adjust the limit dynamically: probe slightly above the current limit, and if latency degrades, back off; if latency is flat, inch up. It's TCP congestion control re-applied to service concurrency — the system continuously discovers its own μ instead of hardcoding a guess. When an interviewer asks "how do you size the queue bound?", "adaptively, from measured latency" is the senior answer.

Where the queue should live (the one rule)

Across every system above, one rule repeats: let the backlog accumulate in the cheapest, most purpose-built place. Kafka lag lives on broker disk, not in consumer RAM. The presale's waiting room holds ticket numbers (a few bytes each), not open checkout connections. TCP's window stops the sender instead of buffering at the receiver. Whenever you draw a queue on a whiteboard, ask: is this the cheapest place this wait could live? If the answer is "in 400,000 open HTTP connections", redraw it.

05

When backpressure goes wrong — failure modes

Every lever misapplied becomes an incident. These are the four ways backpressure designs actually fail in production — each with the mechanism, the math, and the fix.

1. The unbounded buffer — death by "just queue it"

The most common failure: a queue with no bound, in a system where λ > μ for longer than anyone expected. The queue grows, memory grows, garbage-collection pauses grow, latency grows — and then the process dies of out-of-memory, taking the queued work with it. The cruel part: the queue was the protection, and it became the bomb. Fix: every queue gets a bound; when the bound is hit, the system sheds or pushes back (levers 3 and 4). "Unbounded queue" should read as "unbounded incident" in every design review.

2. The retry storm — when the cure multiplies the disease

A client calls your overloaded checkout; it times out after 30 seconds; the client retries. Then retries the retry. If each failed request is retried 3 times, one unit of demand becomes four — and if the failure rate is r, the effective load multiplier is the geometric series:

multiplier = 1 + r + r² + r³ + … (up to max retries)
r = 20% failures, 3 retries → 1 + 0.2 + 0.04 + 0.008 = 1.25× load
r = 50% failures, 3 retries → 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 = 1.88× load

Read the second line: at 50% failure, naive retries nearly double the load — which raises the failure rate — which raises the multiplier. This is the retry storm (also called a thundering herd when it's synchronized: 10,000 clients all retrying at the same instant, often because they share one timeout value). The surge didn't kill the system; the response to the surge did. Try it in Lab B.

The fix has four parts, all required:

  • Backoff — wait longer between retries: 1s, 2s, 4s, 8s (exponential backoff). Spreads the retry load over time.
  • Jitter — add randomness to the backoff (±50%). Without jitter, every client that failed at 10:00:00 retries at 10:00:01 — synchronized, a herd. With jitter, they scatter across the second.
  • Retry budget — cap retries as a fraction of total traffic (e.g., retries ≤ 10% of requests). When the budget is spent, fail fast instead of retrying. This bounds the multiplier no matter what clients do.
  • Never retry what the server already refused for overload. A 503 with Retry-After means wait the stated time; a 429 means back off. Retrying either immediately is the client attacking the server it's trying to use.

3. Backpressure that doesn't propagate — the bottleneck moves

You add a beautiful bounded queue with shedding at the API gateway. Traffic shifts — and now the database connection pool is the unbounded queue, and nobody put a bound on it. Backpressure applied at one layer while the layer below has no protection just moves the explosion downstream. Fix: trace the full path of a request and put the protection at every layer that can saturate: gateway queue bound, service thread-pool bound, DB pool max connections with a wait timeout, downstream client timeouts with budgets. The chain is only as protected as its least-protected link.

4. Shedding the wrong work — protecting the system, losing the business

Under pressure, the easiest thing to shed is whatever arrives most — which is often the revenue. A presale that sheds checkouts but keeps serving seat-map browsers has protected its servers and bankrupted its quarter. Fix: the shedding order is a business decision written down before the incident: bots first, then anonymous browsing, then non-critical features — the money path (payment, checkout commit) last, and even then with clean failure semantics (release holds, clear messaging). If you can't name your shedding order in the room, you don't have one.

⚽ The four stadium disasters, translated: (1) Letting the stairwells fill with no limit until the structure fails — the unbounded buffer. (2) Announcing "gates are full" and having the entire crowd surge back at once, repeatedly — the retry storm. (3) Controlling the outer gate while the inner concourse has no stewards at all — unpropagated backpressure. (4) Ejecting the season-ticket holders to make room for the people selling scarves outside — shedding the wrong work.

Cascading failure: why this lesson matters beyond one service

Cascading failure is what happens when one saturated service drags its callers down with it: Service A slows down → Service B's threads all block waiting on A → B stops serving its callers → C blocks on B → the whole graph freezes, even though only A was sick. Backpressure is the circuit-breaker of this cascade at the demand level: B shedding/pushing back on its callers keeps B's threads free, so B survives A's slowness. (A circuit breaker is the related but different mechanism: B stops calling the sick A for a while and fails fast instead — it protects the caller from a broken dependency; backpressure protects a service from too much demand. They compose: breaker cuts demand to the sick dependency; backpressure cuts demand from your own callers.)

06

Senior-depth traps

Three places where confident-sounding answers fall apart. If you can navigate these, you own the topic.

  1. "Just add a queue" is not a backpressure strategy — it's a backpressure postponement. A queue doesn't change λ or μ; it just converts "refused now" into "waiting, then refused later, with interest" unless the imbalance is temporary. The follow-up question is always: what happens when the queue fills? If the answer is "it can't fill", you've drawn an unbounded queue — see failure mode 1.
  2. Retries are load — budget them like load. Most designs specify retry counts ("3 retries") without specifying retry budgets. Under overload, the retry policy is the load policy: 3 naive retries at 50% failure nearly doubles demand. The senior move is naming all four parts — backoff, jitter, budget, and honoring Retry-After — and being able to write the multiplier math from section 05 on the board.
  3. Push-back assumes a cooperative client; the internet is not cooperative. request(n) works between your own services. For the public internet, pair every push-back with enforcement: 429s with per-client limits for the well-behaved, shedding for the bots and the refresh-mashers. And remember the cheapest backpressure of all is admission-shaped demand: the waiting room that admits exactly μ per second never needs to shed anyone — the queue is ticket numbers, not connections.

09

Q&A with full model answers

Four questions in the shape they get asked, each with the answer a senior is expected to give — including the numbers, the trade-offs named out loud, and the follow-up handled before it's asked.

Q1 · Classic design
Design the derby ticket presale: 500K fans at 10 AM, 40K seats, checkout capacity 300/sec.
They want the overload design, not the happy path. Start with the math, then the mechanism.

Math first: 500K fans in the first 60 seconds ≈ 8,333 arrivals/sec against 300 checkouts/sec — ρ ≈ 28. The queue would grow at ~8,000/sec and the last fan would wait ~27 minutes by Little's Law. No buffer absorbs that; the design has to shape the demand.

Core mechanism — a virtual waiting room: at 10:00 every fan gets a ticket number (a few bytes of state, cheap) and a position. Admission into checkout runs at exactly μ — 300/sec — so checkout connections never exceed capacity and the queue depth in the expensive tier stays flat. The page shows position + honest ETA (position ÷ 300/sec). Refreshing doesn't lose your place (ticket number in a cookie/localStorage, idempotent).

Around the room: (1) Bot defenses first — presales attract scalpers; rate-limit aggressive clients and challenge suspicious ones before they get ticket numbers, so the room holds humans. (2) Idempotent checkout — one ticket number, one seat hold, one charge; retries can't double-book (the outbox/idempotency-key pattern from Lesson 21). (3) Payment provider limits — the provider caps you too; the admission rate must respect the provider's rate limit, not just yours. (4) Degrade the page — static seat map, no recommendations during the sale. (5) Clean failure — if a checkout fails mid-payment, release the seat hold and say so; never a silent drop on the money path.

Why not just autoscale? Frontends scale in minutes; the surge peaks in 60 seconds. And the database writes and the payment provider don't autoscale at all — scaling moves the bottleneck to the payment API. The waiting room is the design; scaling is the pre-planned supplement.

Handle the follow-up ("what if fans complain about the wait?"): the wait is honest and bounded — position and ETA are visible, and a 20-minute known wait with a held place converts far better than a 27-minute spinning loader that times out. Backpressure you can see is a feature.

Q2 · Trade-off judgment
Your service is overloaded — λ > μ and the queue is growing. Walk me through your options.
The five-lever question. Name all five, then argue which dominate.

Frame it: "λ exceeds μ, so the queue grows at (λ−μ)/sec. I have five levers, and I'd combine them: push back, shed, degrade, buffer, scale."

  1. Push back first — 429s with Retry-After for well-behaved clients; the signal coordinates the slowdown instead of letting clients guess.
  2. Shed by value — 503 the lowest-value work first (scrapers, bots, expensive optional features); protect the money path; every shed fails fast and loud with a retry time.
  3. Degrade — serve cached/static answers, turn off personalization and recommendations; this directly raises μ per server.
  4. Buffer what's left — with a hard bound sized for the burst I expect (seconds, not minutes); when the bound hits, I'm back to shedding, by design.
  5. Scale in parallel — it's the slowest lever (minutes to boot, and the DB/payment tier may not scale at all), so it's pre-planned, not improvised.

What I'd watch: queue growth rate and consumer lag, not just depth — a stable deep queue is fine, a growing one is the incident. And I'd check the retry policy immediately, because naive retries multiply the load (the geometric-series math) and can turn my shedding into a retry storm.

The senior close: "And I'd put the same protection at every layer that can saturate — gateway, thread pools, DB connection pool — because backpressure at one layer just moves the explosion to the next unprotected one."

Q3 · Concepts
How does backpressure differ from a circuit breaker? When would you use each?
They're testing whether you conflate two resilience patterns. Don't.

The one-line distinction: a circuit breaker protects you from a sick dependency; backpressure protects you from too much demand. Different direction, different problem.

Circuit breaker: Service B calls Service A; A gets slow. B's breaker trips after N failures/timeouts and B stops calling A for a cooldown period, failing fast instead. It protects B's threads from being consumed by a broken downstream, and it gives A room to recover. The signal flows downstream-to-upstream about a dependency's health.

Backpressure: too many callers hit B. B sheds, 429s, or queues-with-a-bound to protect itself. The signal flows upstream about B's own capacity.

They compose: in a cascade, you want both — B's breaker cuts demand to the sick A, while B's backpressure cuts demand from B's own callers, so B's threads stay free and the failure stays contained at A instead of freezing the whole call graph. If an interviewer asks for "resilience patterns", naming the pair — and the direction each signal flows — is the answer.

Q4 · Mechanisms
Explain pull-based backpressure: TCP flow control and reactive streams' request(n).
They want the mechanism, precisely — this is the "do you actually know how it works" question.

The idea: in push-based systems the sender decides the pace and the receiver copes. In pull-based backpressure the slow side sets the pace: demand flows upstream before data flows downstream, so the receiver can never be overwhelmed — the queue, if any, lives on the producer's side, which is the designed-for-it place.

TCP flow control: the receiver advertises a receive window — "I have room for N bytes" — in every ACK. The sender is forbidden from having more than N unacknowledged bytes in flight. Slow receiver → window shrinks → sender slows → window hits zero → sender stops entirely until the receiver reopens it. No buffering explosion, no loss; the protocol enforces the slow side's pace.

Reactive request(n): the subscriber sends request(n) — "send me exactly n items" — and the publisher may send at most n. When the subscriber finishes processing, it requests more. Same shape as TCP's window, one level up: the consumer's readiness, not the producer's eagerness, drives the flow. Kafka's consumer pause is the same idea applied to partitions — fall behind, stop fetching, let lag sit on cheap broker disk.

The design lesson: whenever you connect a fast producer to a slower consumer — an event firehose, a fan-out worker, a stream processor — prefer the pull shape. Ask "who sets the pace here?" and make sure the answer is the slower side.

Practice drills (untimed)

Work these on paper or a whiteboard. Each has a rubric — grade yourself honestly.

Drill 1 · Capacity math
An API receives λ = 2,000 req/s against μ = 500 req/s, with a bounded queue capped at 10,000. (a) How long until the queue fills and shedding starts? (b) What's the wait for a request arriving just as the queue fills? (c) The team proposes "just make the cap 100,000". What's wrong with that?
Rubric — check your answer
  • (a) Growth = 2,000 − 500 = 1,500/s → 10,000 ÷ 1,500 ≈ 6.7 seconds. Full marks for showing the (λ−μ) step, not just the number.
  • (b) Little's Law: W = 10,000 ÷ 500 = 20 seconds. Bonus for noting 20s likely exceeds the client timeout — so much of the buffered work will be retried or abandoned anyway.
  • (c) The cap isn't the problem — the imbalance duration is. 100K cap fills in 67s and the last arrival waits 200s. Bigger queue = bigger memory + longer waits + more stale work, with no change to λ or μ. Full marks for: "a queue is a bet the imbalance is temporary; raising the cap just raises the stakes of the bet."
Drill 2 · Incident autopsy
10:00 — your checkout API starts returning 503s under a traffic spike. The mobile client retries each failed request 3 times, immediately. 10:05 — the entire checkout tier is down, including the database. Diagnose the cascade and write the four-part fix.
Rubric — check your answer
  • Diagnosis: retry storm. At rising failure rates the 3× immediate retries multiply effective load (1 + r + r² + r³), which raises r further — a feedback loop. The DB died because the multiplied load hit the tier that couldn't shed. Full marks for naming the loop, not just "too much traffic".
  • Fix (all four): exponential backoff + jitter on retries; a retry budget (retries capped as a fraction of traffic); honor Retry-After on 503/429 instead of retrying immediately; bounded queues + shedding at the tier above the DB so the database is never the component that discovers the overload.
  • Bonus: note the client timeout (probably 30s+) meant doomed requests held connections the whole time — shortening timeouts with backoff reduces the blast radius.
Drill 3 · Cross-lesson design
Apply this lesson to Lesson 22's notification system: the cup-final goal just fired, 80M pushes need sending in 60s, and the push provider starts 429ing you. Write the shedding order and the backpressure wiring, stage by stage.
Rubric — check your answer
  • Shedding order (business-driven): digest/marketing lane paused first → standard lane degraded into digests → engagement (goal alerts) protected → transactional (OTP) never shed. Full marks for justifying the order by value-per-message and reversibility.
  • Wiring: provider 429s honored with Retry-After (never retried immediately); per-channel queues are bounded with overflow → shed lowest lane first; workers use backoff + jitter; queue-depth growth rate alerts before depth does; preference-check stays upstream of everything (never skipped to "save capacity" — the fines cost more than the servers).
  • Bonus: the fan-out worker chunks at 10K users so the surge enters the queues in a controlled ramp, not one 62M-row explosion — admission shaping, the waiting-room idea from this lesson.

Cheat sheet, 60-second framing & talk-track

The whole lesson on one screen. Read this the morning of.

Backpressure — six decisions

  1. Measure λ and μ first. You can't push back what you can't see. Dashboard queue depth and growth rate; alert on the growth rate — a stable deep queue is fine, a growing one is the incident.
  2. Buffers absorb seconds, not minutes. Bound every queue; size the bound for the burst you expect; define what happens when it fills (shed/push back) before it fills.
  3. Shed by value, not by arrival order. Bots and scrapers first, optional features next, the money path last — and the shedding order is a business decision written down before the incident.
  4. Push back early and explicitly. 429/503 with Retry-After, not silent timeouts. A coordinated slowdown beats a thousand clients guessing.
  5. Retries are load: budget them. Backoff + jitter + retry budget + honor Retry-After. Know the multiplier math (1 + r + r² + …) cold.
  6. Scale is the slowest lever. Minutes to boot, and DB writes / payment providers don't scale with you. Design stateless and partitionable so the lever exists — but never improvise it mid-surge.

60-second framing (say it verbatim to open): "Backpressure is what a system does when demand outruns capacity — when the arrival rate λ exceeds the service rate μ. The queue then grows at (λ−μ) per second, and by Little's Law the wait grows with it. There are five responses: buffer the overflow with a hard bound, scale capacity up, shed the lowest-value work with fast 503s, push back on senders with 429s and Retry-After, or degrade to cheaper answers. The failure modes are an unbounded buffer, a retry storm that multiplies the load, backpressure that doesn't propagate past one layer, and shedding the revenue instead of the bots. My rule: let the backlog live in the cheapest place — ticket numbers, not connections; broker disk, not consumer memory."

Name the failure mode you're preventing: "…so we don't get a retry storm / an unbounded queue / a cascade into the database." 4. Close with the cheap-place rule: "and the backlog lives as ticket numbers, not open connections." Four sentences, senior signal in each one.
