Distributed Transactions & the Saga Pattern

One checkout, three databases: the seat gets reserved in one service, the card is charged in another, the confirmation goes out from a third. If the charge fails after the seat is reserved, you can't leave the books half-written — but there is no shared transaction to roll back. Two-phase commit is the textbook answer and the production trap; the saga pattern is what the industry actually runs.

01

What is a distributed transaction?

The whole lesson in one scene: a fan buys a season ticket on the Riverside FC app. One tap must do three things — reserve seat 14B in the Seats service, charge $240 in the Payments service, and send the confirmation from the Notifications service. Each service owns its own database. What does "all or nothing" mean when there is no single database to roll back?

Distributed transaction

A business transaction that writes to two or more independent data stores, and must either commit in all of them or in none of them. "Independent" is doing all the work in that sentence: each store has its own storage engine, its own failure modes, and no shared undo log with the others.

ACID, from zero

Inside one database you get four guarantees so cheaply you forget they're there — the acronym is ACID:

  • Atomicity — all or nothing. BEGIN; UPDATE seats …; UPDATE payments …; COMMIT; Either both writes land or neither does. A crash halfway is not a half-written ticket.
  • Consistency — the data's rules hold. Foreign keys, check constraints, "a seat can't be reserved twice" — the database refuses to commit a state that breaks them.
  • Isolation — no peeking mid-flight. While your transaction runs, other readers see either the before-state or the after-state, never the half-written middle.
  • Durability — once committed, it survives a crash. The write is on disk (write-ahead log) before the database says "done".

One database, one transaction, ACID for free. That's the baseline every alternative in this lesson is compared against.

What microservices break

Microservices exist precisely to give each team its own database — independent deploys, independent scaling, no shared schema to coordinate. But that ownership draws a hard line around each transaction: a transaction cannot cross a database boundary. When the season-ticket checkout needs seats + payments + notifications to agree, you're outside ACID's jurisdiction.

The failure you're defending against is the partial write: seat 14B reserved, card charged, confirmation email lost to a crash — or worse, card charged, seat not reserved, and a very angry fan. Every pattern in this lesson is a different answer to the same question: who restores "all or nothing" when the database can't?

⚽ The soccer picture to keep

Think of the three services as three match officials, each with their own notebook. The referee can't erase the linesman's notes — there's no shared eraser. If the fourth official's notebook blows away in the wind mid-decision, the other three have to agree on how to fix the record using only their own notebooks. Every distributed-transaction pattern is a rulebook for that agreement.

02

The textbook answer: two-phase commit (2PC)

The oldest answer is also the most literal: if you can't roll back together, at least promise together. Two-phase commit adds a referee — the transaction coordinator (also called the transaction manager) — and splits every transaction into a promise phase and a commit phase.

How it works, step by step

Season-ticket checkout under 2PC, with the coordinator talking to three participants (Seats, Payments, Notifications):

  1. Phase 1 — prepare. The coordinator asks each participant: "Can you commit this?" Each one runs its checks (seat still free? card valid?), writes its intended change to a durable prepare log on its own disk, acquires the locks on the affected rows — and only then replies YES. A NO (or a timeout) from anyone kills the transaction.
  2. Phase 2 — commit or abort. If every participant voted yes, the coordinator durably records the decision, then tells everyone COMMIT. Each participant commits its prepared change and releases its locks. If anyone voted no, the coordinator sends ABORT and everyone discards its prepared change.

The protocol's single honest idea: nobody commits until everybody has promised they can. The promise is durable (it's in the prepare log), so a participant crash after voting yes doesn't break anything — it replays its prepared state on recovery and waits for the coordinator's decision.

The two fatal problems

Problem 1 — blocking. Between voting YES and hearing the decision, a participant holds its locks and cannot decide on its own. It promised. If the coordinator crashes in that window, every participant sits there — locks held, seat 14B frozen, other checkouts queuing behind it — until the coordinator comes back. Recovery can't be unilateral: the only safe fix is for participants to agree on what the coordinator would have decided, and agreeing after a crash is exactly the consensus problem from Lesson 14. 2PC buys atomicity and pays in availability.

Problem 2 — the coordinator is a single point of failure, and the protocol is slow. Every transaction costs at least two cross-network round trips plus durable log writes before anything commits. At microservice scale, that's latency on the critical path and a component whose death freezes everyone mid-transaction.

This is why 2PC survives in exactly one habitat: inside a single vendor's cluster — a database's own replication, or the old XA standard where one bank's mainframe coordinated one bank's databases, all under one operator's control. Across independently-operated services on the open network, the industry walked away from it decades ago. Try it in Lab B — the blocking is not subtle.

Blocking protocol, defined

A protocol is blocking if the failure of one component can leave the others unable to make progress, waiting indefinitely for it to recover. 2PC is the canonical example; the saga pattern in section 03 is explicitly designed to be non-blocking: every participant can always move forward — either to the next step or to a compensation.

03

The saga pattern — from zero

The saga flips the problem. Instead of preventing partial failure with promises and locks, it accepts partial failure and designs the repair. A saga breaks one business transaction into a sequence of local transactions — T1, T2, T3 — each atomic inside its own service's database. If T2 fails, the saga runs compensating transactions — C1 — that semantically undo T1. Forward progress, never blocking.

Saga

A sequence of local transactions (each with its own ACID guarantees) that together form one business transaction. Each step has a defined compensating transaction — a new write that semantically reverses the step's effect. On failure, compensations run in reverse order (C3, C2, C1), like unwinding a stack.

The season ticket as a saga

  • T1 — Reserve seat. Seats service marks 14B reserved. Compensation C1: release the seat.
  • T2 — Charge card. Payments service charges $240. Compensation C2: refund $240.
  • T3 — Confirm. Notifications service sends the email, order marked complete. Compensation C3: n/a — but see section 04 for why this step is last.

If T2 fails (card declined), the saga runs C1 — releases the seat — and the order dies cleanly. No locks held across services, no coordinator to block on: each service only ever touches its own database.

Two flavors: choreography vs orchestration

The steps are the same; who drives differs.

  • Choreography — no boss, just events. Each service does its local transaction and publishes an event: SeatReserved → Payments hears it, charges, publishes CardCharged → Notifications hears it, sends the email. Nobody owns the whole flow; the business transaction emerges from services reacting to each other. Decoupled and simple for 2–3 steps — but try to answer "where is order #4821 right now?" and you're chasing events across logs. The compensation logic is also scattered: who decides a refund is owed lives in whoever heard the failure event.
  • Orchestration — one conductor. A dedicated saga orchestrator (a service, or a durable-workflow engine) issues commands — ReserveSeat, then ChargeCard, then ConfirmOrder — tracks the saga's state machine, and decides compensations when a step fails. The flow is explicit, inspectable, and debuggable: order #4821's state lives in one place. The price is a new service you must build, scale, and keep available — though note it can always make progress (retry, compensate), so its failure degrades rather than blocks.

Which one, in the room?

Few steps, stable flow, simple compensations → choreography. The coupling cost is low and there's no conductor to operate. Many steps, conditional branches, complex or conditional compensations, or any need for a dashboard/audit of "where is this order" → orchestration. At 5+ steps with branching, choreography's emergent flow becomes genuinely undebuggable — this is the single most common senior-level opinion to state plainly.

04

Compensating transactions are not rollbacks

This is the section that separates a junior answer ("then you roll back") from a senior one. A compensation is a new write that semantically reverses the effect — it never erases history the way a database rollback does.

Four things that make compensations different

  1. They're semantic inverses, not bitwise inverses. Reserve seat → release seat. Charge $240 → refund $240. The ledger shows both movements; the audit trail sees a charge and a refund. A rollback pretends T1 never happened; a compensation admits it happened and repairs the world. (This is exactly what your bank statement shows you in real life — refunds are new lines, never deletions.)
  2. Some steps can't be compensated — so order them last. You cannot unsend an email, un-fire a webhook, or un-ship a package. The ordering rule: put irreversible or expensive-to-reverse steps last, cheap reversible steps first. In the ticket saga, the email goes last because its "compensation" is an embarrassing follow-up email. More generally: validate everything compensable before you touch anything irreversible.
  3. There is no isolation — design for the visible middle. Between T1 and T3, other readers see the intermediate state: the seat shows as reserved-but-unpaid. Unlike a database transaction, you cannot hide the middle. So model it explicitly: seat states like reserving → reserved → confirmed, order states like pending_payment, and make every UI/API treat them as first-class. The half-written state is not a bug you can lock away; it's a state your system must render correctly.
  4. Prefer forward recovery over backward compensation. If T3 (send email) fails with a transient SMTP error, the correct move is retry T3 — not refund the fan and release their seat. Compensate only when the business outcome is actually dead (card declined, seat gone). This is a judgment call per step: retryable infrastructure failure → retry; business-rule failure → compensate.

The compensation that fails. Compensations are writes, and writes can fail — the refund API is down while you're trying to refund. The rule: compensations must be retryable and idempotent (safe to run twice — refund with an idempotency key, release-seat as an idempotent state transition). Retry with backoff; if it still won't go, the saga parks the order in a needs_manual_review state and pages a human. The honest senior line: sagas don't eliminate the unfixable case, they shrink it to a monitored queue instead of a frozen database.

05

Making sagas survivable: the transactional outbox

Sagas run on events and commands — which means the saga's weakest joint is the seam between writing to the database and telling anyone about it. Get this wrong and steps silently never happen.

The atomicity trap, stated plainly

Inside the Seats service, the naive code does two things:

UPDATE seats SET status='reserved' WHERE id='14B';   -- write 1: the database
publish("SeatReserved", {seat: "14B"});                  -- write 2: the broker

Crash between the two and the seat is reserved but no event ever fires — the saga stalls with nobody notified. Swap the order (publish, then write) and a crash sends a phantom SeatReserved for a seat that was never reserved. Two writes, no atomicity — it is the distributed-transaction problem again, one level down.

The outbox pattern, from zero

Transactional outbox

Instead of publishing directly, the service writes the event into an outbox table in the same local database transaction as the business write. One atomic commit covers both. A separate relay process then reads unsent outbox rows, publishes them to the broker, and marks them sent.

BEGIN;
  UPDATE seats SET status='reserved' WHERE id='14B';
  INSERT INTO outbox(event_id, type, payload) VALUES ('evt-991', 'SeatReserved', '{...}');
COMMIT;   -- both or neither. This is the whole trick.

The relay can be a simple poller (SELECT … WHERE sent=false) or log-based CDC (change-data-capture, e.g. Debezium tailing the write-ahead log — no polling load on the database). Either way, crash-safety falls out naturally:

  • Crash before commit → nothing happened, nothing published. Correct.
  • Crash after commit, before relay publishes → the outbox row is still sent=false; the relay publishes it on restart. Correct.
  • Crash after publish, before marking sent → the relay publishes again. Duplicate event — which is why the next paragraph exists.

Idempotent consumers close the loop

The relay's contract is at-least-once delivery — the third case above makes duplicates possible by construction, and "exactly-once" across a database and a broker is not on offer (same impossibility flavor as Lesson 9's delivery semantics). So every consumer must be idempotent: safe to process the same event twice. The standard recipe is an event_id primary key on the consumer side — INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING — or a conditional state transition (UPDATE … WHERE status='pending'). At-least-once delivery + idempotent consumers = the only sane contract. Say that sentence verbatim in the room; it's the password.

Why this composes with everything you've learned: the outbox is Lesson 9's queue semantics applied to the producer side; the relay's polling/backoff is Lesson 11's territory; idempotency keys are the same idea as Lesson 13's fencing tokens — a unique token that makes a repeated operation safe. Distributed transactions aren't a new topic so much as the place where all the old topics meet.

08

The industry spectrum: how people actually avoid the problem

Sagas are the workhorse, but the senior move is knowing the full menu — including the options that make the problem disappear before it starts.

Option 0: don't have a distributed transaction

The cheapest distributed transaction is the one you design away. Colocate data that must change atomically inside one service and one database — if two writes genuinely cannot tolerate a visible middle, they belong in the same local transaction. Split sagas along natural business seams, and accept eventual consistency wherever the business allows it (analytics, notifications, search indexes). Every cross-service transaction you avoid is 2PC's blocking tax and a saga's compensation graph you never have to build.

Option 1: saga (choreography or orchestration)

The default for cross-service business transactions: e-commerce checkout, booking flows, onboarding. Non-blocking, no distributed locks, compensations as first-class business logic. The cost is the visible middle (section 04) and the operational burden of the compensations themselves.

Option 2: TCC — try / confirm / cancel

TCC (Try–Confirm–Cancel)

Each service exposes three operations: try (tentatively hold the resource — the airline "hold this seat for 15 minutes"), confirm (finalize all holds), cancel (release all holds). Like a saga where every step's "compensation" is designed in from the start as a first-class cancel, and the try-phase holds are time-bounded so nothing stays reserved forever.

TCC shines where business language already has "hold" in it — travel booking, ticket sales, inventory reservation. It's really a saga with a reservation-flavored vocabulary and TTLs on the tentative state.

Option 3: 2PC — only inside one vendor's walls

Keep it where the coordinator and participants share an operator, a network, and a pager rotation: a database's internal replication, XA across one company's mainframe databases. The moment the participants are independently deployed services, the blocking tax (Lab B) disqualifies it.

Where this shows up in real systems

  • E-commerce checkout — orchestrated saga: reserve inventory → charge → arrange shipping, with release/refund/cancel compensations.
  • Hotel + flight booking — TCC: hold both, confirm both, or cancel both; holds expire so a crashed client can't pin inventory forever.
  • Durable orchestrators — workflow engines like Temporal/Cadence exist precisely to run saga orchestrators as crash-safe, event-sourced state machines: the orchestrator itself becomes the thing that can't be lost.
  • Uber-style dispatch (Lesson 19) — saga-flavored: soft-reserve a driver with a TTL, confirm on accept, release on timeout. The TTL is the compensation for a lost message.
09

Q&A with full model answers

Read the question, form your own answer, then open the model answer and compare — especially the reasoning chain, not just the verdict.

Question 1 of 4
Your checkout spans inventory, payment, and shipping. The business rule: never take the customer's money without a reserved item. Saga or 2PC — walk me through your choice.
Model answer

Saga, orchestrated — and 2PC is disqualified before we start. 2PC would give true atomicity, but its participants block holding locks if the coordinator crashes mid-protocol (Lab B), and it adds two round trips plus durable prepare-log writes to the checkout critical path. Across three independently deployed services, that's an availability and latency tax the business didn't ask for.

Step order follows the reversibility rule: T1 reserve inventory (cheap to compensate: release the hold), T2 charge payment (compensable but expensive and customer-visible: refund), T3 create shipping label (irreversible-ish: cancel the label). Payment is never first — its compensation is a refund the customer actually sees on their statement.

Failure handling: T1 fails → nothing to compensate, order dies. T2 fails (declined) → run C1, release the inventory hold. T3 fails → retry first (label APIs flake); only if the business outcome is dead do we refund + release. Every service-to-service handoff goes through the transactional outbox so no step is silently lost, and every consumer is idempotent on event ID because delivery is at-least-once.

The one-line justification: the business needs eventual all-or-nothing with a correct visible middle, not instant all-or-nothing with blocking locks — that's exactly the saga's trade.

Question 2 of 4
A saga's charge step succeeded but the confirmation step failed permanently. The customer was charged. What does your system do — and what does the customer see?
Model answer

Do not refund. The business outcome is correct — a paid, reserved seat — and refunding a good payment because a notification failed is the machine undoing a correct result. Compensations must follow business value, not mechanical reversal.

What the system does: forward recovery first — the confirmation step is retryable (SMTP 5xx, template service down), so retry with backoff via the outbox relay. The order sits in an explicit intermediate state, confirmed_unnotified, which is a first-class state the system knows how to render. If retries exhaust, the order parks in a dead-letter/ops queue with an alert; a daily reconciliation job diffs "charged" against "notified" and catches anything the retries missed.

What the customer sees: their order history shows "confirmed" — because it is confirmed. The email arrives late, or they see the confirmation in-app. What they must never see is a charge with no order, which is the failure mode this design exists to prevent.

The general principle: distinguish retryable infrastructure failure (retry the step) from business-rule failure (compensate the saga). Most saga "failures" in production are the first kind.

Question 3 of 4
Why can't the saga just lock the rows it touches until the whole thing completes?
Model answer

Because cross-database locks need a lock coordinator — and that coordinator is 2PC wearing a different hat. A lock held in the Seats database means nothing to the Payments database unless some protocol makes them agree on lock acquisition order, deadlock detection, and what happens when the lock holder crashes. Designing that protocol from scratch re-derives two-phase commit, blocking tax included.

The saga's entire point is trading isolation for availability: instead of preventing the visible middle with locks, it models the visible middle as explicit states (reserving, pending_payment) and repairs bad endings with compensations. If the business truly needs hard isolation — no reader may ever see the intermediate state — then you don't have a saga-shaped problem: colocate the data in one database and use a local transaction (Option 0 in section 08).

One nuance worth volunteering: within each step, the local transaction still uses normal row locks — isolation is abandoned only between steps, never inside one.

Question 4 of 4
Choreography or orchestration for a 6-step travel booking flow — flight, hotel, car, payment, insurance, email — with partial-refund rules that depend on which steps succeeded?
Model answer

Orchestration, without hesitation. Three reasons, in order of importance:

  1. The compensation graph is conditional. Partial-refund rules ("refund flight only if hotel was never confirmed") are business logic that needs one place to live. In choreography that logic scatters across six services' event handlers, and the question "what is the correct refund for booking #7712?" has no single place to be answered.
  2. Debuggability at six steps with branches. An orchestrator keeps the saga's state machine in one durable store — you can open a dashboard and see exactly which step booking #7712 is on. Choreography's emergent flow is fine at two steps and genuinely undebuggable at six with conditional branches.
  3. Retry policy centralization. Flight APIs and hotel APIs fail differently; the orchestrator holds per-step retry/backoff policy instead of each service inventing its own.

The honest caveat to add: the orchestrator is now a service I must keep available — but it degrades, it doesn't block: its state is a durable state machine (a database row + outbox, or a workflow engine like Temporal), so a crash just means "resume on restart," never "everyone freezes holding locks." That's the fundamental asymmetry with 2PC's coordinator.

Practice drills (untimed)

Untimed, on paper or in your head. The rubric under each drill is the actual grading bar — check yourself against it.

Drill 1
Saga the food-delivery order
A food-delivery order has five steps: accept the order, charge the customer, assign a courier, get the restaurant's confirmation, dispatch the courier. List each local transaction, write its compensating transaction, and order the steps so the hardest-to-reverse goes last. Then name the failure at each step and say whether you'd retry or compensate.
Rubric — what a strong answer includes
  • Steps ordered by reversibility: accept → charge → assign courier → restaurant confirm → dispatch last (dispatch is the irreversible one — the courier is physically moving).
  • Every step has a named compensation: accept→cancel order record; charge→refund; assign→unassign; restaurant confirm→cancel with restaurant; dispatch→recall is best-effort, so dispatch must be last and guarded.
  • Retry-vs-compensate judgment per step: transient failures (payment gateway timeout, courier-service 500) → retry; business failures (card declined, restaurant rejects) → compensate.
  • Names the visible middle: between "charged" and "restaurant confirmed," the customer sees "finding your restaurant…" — an explicit state, not a spinner of unknown meaning.
Drill 2
Choreography or orchestration — three calls
Decide the driving style for each, with one sentence of justification: (a) signup flow — create user, send welcome email; (b) insurance claim — 5 steps with fraud checks and conditional payouts; (c) marketplace payout — split one payment across 3 sellers with per-seller tax withholding.
Rubric — what a strong answer includes
  • (a) Choreography — two steps, linear, trivially compensable (delete user / nothing to undo for the email). A conductor would be pure overhead.
  • (b) Orchestration — conditional branches and fraud-check outcomes need centralized state and a single place for the payout rules; debugging emergent events across 5 steps is the failure mode.
  • (c) Orchestration — money movement with per-party rules needs an auditable, replayable state machine; "who got paid what" must be answerable from one store.
  • Bonus credit: notes that (a) could graduate to orchestration if a third step with branching (e.g., KYC verification) is added later — the choice follows complexity, not dogma.
Drill 3
Sketch the outbox
Design the outbox table for the Payments service in the ticket saga, plus the relay that publishes to the broker. Then answer: what exactly breaks if the relay dies for 10 minutes? What breaks if it delivers an event twice?
Rubric — what a strong answer includes
  • Table sketch: outbox(event_id PK, aggregate_id, type, payload JSON, created_at, sent_at NULL); business write + outbox insert in one local transaction.
  • Relay: polls WHERE sent_at IS NULL ORDER BY created_at (or CDC on the WAL), publishes, then marks sent. Mentions the publish-then-mark ordering and why it forces at-least-once.
  • Relay dead 10 min: nothing breaks, only delays — charges are durable in the DB; events queue up in the outbox and flush on recovery. The saga pauses; it doesn't corrupt. (Contrast: naive publish-then-crash would have lost steps.)
  • Delivered twice: nothing breaks if consumers are idempotent — dedupe on event_id (insert-if-absent) or conditional state transitions. Names the sentence: at-least-once + idempotent consumers is the only sane contract.
11

Cheat sheet & 60-second framing

Six decisions, in order

  1. Can the writes live in one database? If yes, stop — a local transaction beats every pattern here.
  2. 2PC or saga? 2PC only inside one vendor's cluster. Across services: saga. The blocker is the blocking.
  3. Choreography or orchestration? Few linear steps → choreography. Branching, 5+ steps, or "where is order #4821?" → orchestration.
  4. Order steps by reversibility. Cheap and reversible first; irreversible or customer-visible (refunds, emails, shipments) last.
  5. Retry or compensate? Transient infrastructure failure → retry the step. Business-rule failure → compensate the saga, in reverse.
  6. How do steps talk? Transactional outbox on the producer side; idempotent consumers on the other. At-least-once is the contract — design for it, don't fight it.

60-second framing — say this in the room:

"A distributed transaction is one business action across independent databases, so there's no shared rollback. Two-phase commit gets all-or-nothing by making every participant promise before anyone commits — but if the coordinator crashes mid-protocol, participants block holding locks, which is why the industry doesn't use it across services. The saga pattern breaks the transaction into local steps with compensating transactions that unwind in reverse. I order steps so the hardest to reverse goes last, retry transient failures instead of compensating them, use the transactional outbox so no step is silently lost, and keep every consumer idempotent because delivery is at-least-once."

🎙️ In-the-room talk-track (when they ask you to design checkout)

"I'll model checkout as an orchestrated saga across inventory, payment, and fulfillment. Step order: reserve inventory, then charge, then fulfill — cheapest to reverse first. Every handoff goes through a transactional outbox; consumers dedupe on event IDs. Payment failure releases the hold; fulfillment failure retries first and only refunds if the business outcome is dead. The order has explicit intermediate states so the UI never shows a charge without an order. I considered 2PC and rejected it — a coordinator crash would freeze inventory with locks held, and I don't need instant atomicity, I need eventual all-or-nothing that never blocks."
