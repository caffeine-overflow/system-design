01

What is a ride-hailing platform?

Before any architecture: what the thing actually is, defined from zero, with one running example carried through the whole lesson.

Definition

A ride-hailing platform is a two-sided, real-time marketplace. On one side, riders (phones asking "take me from A to B"); on the other, drivers (phones moving around the city streaming their GPS position every few seconds). The platform's job is four verbs: track every driver's live position, match each rider request to a good driver, price the trip so supply shows up when demand spikes, and follow the trip until the car arrives — all in seconds, for millions of trips a day.

Our running example: Riverside Rides, the ride-hailing service for the metro around Riverside FC's stadium. Normal Tuesday: a few thousand trips an hour spread across the city. Matchday: 25,000 fans pour out of the stadium at 9:50 PM and all want a car within ten minutes. Everything in this lesson is engineered for that second scenario without wasting money on the first.

Soccer analogy

The matchmaker's job

Think of a transfer-window scout network. Every agent (driver) reports where they are every few minutes. When a club (rider) calls "I need a striker in Manchester," the scout doesn't phone every agent in Europe — she narrows to agents in the North West, then to the ones whose striker is available and close, then picks the best fit and quotes a fee that rises when ten clubs are bidding. Tracking, narrowing, matching, pricing. A ride-hailing platform does exactly that, ~30 times a second, for cars.

The vocabulary, fixed now

  • Geospatial query — a query about positions: "find all drivers within 2 km of this point," or "how far is this driver by road?" Everything in this lesson flows through these.
  • ETA (estimated time of arrival) — how long until a driver reaches the rider, and later how long the trip takes. Computed from road-network distance (driving along streets), not straight-line distance — 1 km across a river with no bridge can be 20 minutes.
  • Dispatch — the act of choosing a driver for a rider and sending them the trip. A dispatch loop is the repeated, expanding search for candidates.
  • Surge pricing — temporarily raising prices in an area where demand outruns supply, to pull drivers toward it and ask riders "is this trip worth it right now?"
  • Geohash — a way of turning a (latitude, longitude) pair into a short text string whose shared prefix means "the same area." It becomes the index key that makes "nearby" fast. Fully built from zero in §3.
  • Map matching — snapping noisy GPS points onto actual roads. GPS says the driver is in the middle of the river; map matching knows they're on the bridge.
02

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope sizing

A senior-room design starts here: what must be true, what can be slow, and how big the numbers are — before drawing a single box.

Functional requirements

  • Riders: request a ride (pickup + destination), see nearby cars and a quoted price + ETA, track the arriving car live on a map, pay at the end.
  • Drivers: go online/offline, receive trip offers, navigate to pickup, run the meter, get paid.
  • Platform: match riders to drivers in seconds, price dynamically, keep driver locations fresh enough to be trustworthy.

Non-functional requirements

  • Low latency on match: dispatch should complete in a few seconds — a rider staring at "finding your driver…" for 60 seconds is a lost rider.
  • High availability, but not every corner is equal: ride request and dispatch must never be down; history and receipts can tolerate a blip (a CAP-flavored call you'll make explicitly, like Lesson 7).
  • Consistency where money moves: a trip must have exactly one driver (no double-dispatch), and exactly one charge (idempotent payments).

Back-of-the-envelope (our metro)

The sizing assumptions — write these down first, argue with them later

  • Trips: 1M trips/day in the metro (avg 40K/hr daytime, 5× peaks around events = 200K/hr)
  • Drivers online at peak: 120,000; average: 60,000
  • Location ping: every 4 seconds per active driver (the industry-typical heartbeat)
  • Avg trip: 6 km, 18 minutes; avg fare: $14
  • Location data kept hot for 7 days, then cold storage
  • Location write rate (the dominant load): 120,000 drivers ÷ 4 s = 30,000 pings/sec. Each ping is ~100 bytes (driver id, lat, lng, timestamp, heading, speed) → 3 MB/s, ~260 GB/day. This dwarfs trip writes — the system is really a location ingestion machine with a matching service attached.
  • Trip writes: 1M/day ≈ 12/sec average, ~150/sec at matchday peak. Trivial next to the pings — the database design bends around the writes you don't notice.
  • Match queries: peak 200K trip requests/hr ≈ 55/sec, each triggering an expanding ring search (§5). Cheap individually; the heat is in the location writes.
  • Read fan-out per rider app: the map refresh pulls ~10–20 nearby car markers every few seconds per active rider app. At 300K concurrent rider apps, that's ~1M small reads/sec — which is why "nearby cars" is served from a cache (§3), never from the database.

The one-line sizing verdict: the hot path is 30K location writes/sec + ~1M cached nearby-car reads/sec; trips themselves are a rounding error. If your architecture optimizes for anything other than the location pipeline first, you've designed the wrong system.

03

The heart: finding nearby drivers

Every dispatch starts with one question — "who's close?" — and the naive answer collapses at city scale. This section is the algorithmic core of the lesson.

Why you can't just query the database

The naive design: a drivers table with lat, lng, available, and a query like SELECT * FROM drivers WHERE distance(lat, lng, rider) < 2km. That query must compute the distance to every driver — 120,000 distance calculations per request, at 55 requests/sec during the matchday rush. It never uses an index because "distance from a moving point" isn't indexable by a B-tree (Lesson 8). The database melts.

The fix is an index on space itself: organize drivers by where they are so "nearby" becomes a handful of key lookups instead of a full scan.

Geohash, from zero

A geohash turns a (latitude, longitude) pair into a short string like u4pruy. The construction is what matters:

  1. Take the longitude range (−180…180) and the latitude range (−90…90). Repeatedly halve each range; at each halving, write 1 if the point is in the upper half, 0 if lower.
  2. Interleave the longitude bits and latitude bits into one bit string: 1101001110…
  3. Group into 5-bit chunks and encode each with base32 (0–9, b–z minus a, i, l, o to avoid confusion with digits). Six chunks → a 6-character geohash.

The magic property: each extra character zooms one level in, and points that share a prefix are in the same area. It's a spatial index you can store in any key-value store — drivers keyed by geohash, and "drivers near me" = "look up the handful of geohash keys around me." Stadium-seating analogy: the geohash is the stand/row/seat code — everyone whose ticket starts with U4PRUY is in the same section of the ground, so finding nearby fans is a prefix lookup, not a search of the whole stadium.

Geohash precision table — the one to memorize

CharsCell size (at equator, roughly)Use it for
4~39 km × 20 kmToo coarse for dispatch; fine for analytics regions
5~4.9 km × 4.9 kmCity-zone dispatch: a 5-char cell holds dozens of drivers
6~1.2 km × 0.6 kmPickup-point precision, dense downtowns
7~150 m × 150 mCurbside: which side of the street the car is on

Cell size shrinks toward the poles (meridians converge) — longitude degrees get shorter. Never quote a cell size without the latitude caveat; it comes up in senior rooms.

The boundary trap every candidate should name

Two points 10 meters apart can have completely different geohash prefixes if they sit on opposite sides of a cell boundary — like fans in adjacent rows whose seat codes start with different stand letters. The fix is mechanical: when searching, query the rider's own cell plus its 8 neighbors (a 3×3 block). A 9-cell lookup instead of 1 is the cost of the geohash's simplicity — and it motivates the ring expansion below.

Dispatch via expanding ring search

The standard dispatch algorithm doesn't search one fixed radius. It searches in expanding rings:

  1. Ring 0: the rider's own geohash-5 cell (~25 km²).
  2. No available drivers? Ring 1: the 8 surrounding cells (9 cells total).
  3. Still none? Ring 2: the next layer (25 cells), and so on — up to a max radius (say 15 km or 4 rings), each step wider and each candidate's ETA checked before offering.

Why rings instead of one big radius? Because density varies: downtown, ring 0 holds 40 drivers and you're done in one lookup; at 2 AM in the suburbs, ring 0 holds zero and you widen automatically. One radius either misses drivers or scans half the city. The ring adapts to the map.

The math that makes this cheap: 120,000 drivers spread over a 3,000 km² metro ≈ 40 drivers/km². A 25 km² cell holds ~1,000 drivers (in practice drivers cluster on roads and hotspots, so expect skew — but even 10× skew leaves hundreds per cell). Ring 0 + 8 neighbors = 9 lookups for ~9,000 candidate records. Compared to scanning all 120,000 drivers per request, that's a 10,000× reduction in work. That's the whole reason this section exists.

Quadtree and H3 — the two upgrades to name

Where the alternatives fit

In the room, the safe answer: geohash (or H3) cells as Redis keys for the live driver index; quadtree as the thing you'd reach for if density skew were the stated problem. Uber historically used quadtrees for dispatch storage and H3 for pricing/visualization — naming that split scores senior points.

What the live index actually looks like

Key:   geohash:u4pruy:available      (Redis SET)
Value: driver_id:88412, driver_id:88431, …   (only ONLINE + EMPTY drivers)

On driver ping:  SREM geohash:<old_cell>:available <id>
                 SADD geohash:<new_cell>:available <id>
                 SET  driver:88412:loc  "53.55,-113.49,ts,heading,speed"  EX 30

On rider request: members = SUNION geohash:<cell>:available,
                               geohash:<8 neighbors>:available
                 → fetch each candidate's loc → filter by road-ETA → pick

Notes: a driver's full location is a separate short-TTL key (so a vanished phone stops being dispatched within ~30 s); the SET of "available drivers per cell" is rebuilt purely from the ping stream — the index is a materialized view of the pings, not a second source of truth. Stale reads mean a dispatched driver moved one cell over — acceptable, because the offer round-trip (§5) re-verifies before committing.

04

Ingesting 30,000 location pings a second

The matching algorithm is elegant; the location pipeline is what decides whether the map lies to the rider.

Why not HTTP polling

Each driver's phone holds a long-lived connection to a connection server — the same WebSocket fleet pattern from the chat-system lesson, sized at 500K–1M sockets per box. Every 4 seconds the phone pushes {lat, lng, heading, speed, ts} up the socket. The connection server doesn't touch a database: it fires the ping into a partitioned message stream (Kafka-style topic, partitioned by driver id) and returns. Ingest is a fire-and-forget pipeline: connection → stream → stream processor → Redis index.

Why the stream in the middle, instead of writing Redis directly from the connection server? Two reasons: burst absorption (the final whistle is a 5× write spike; the stream's buffer is the shock absorber — backpressure from the queues lesson) and fan-out (the same ping feeds the dispatch index, the rider-facing map, fraud detection, and the analytics warehouse — pub/sub semantics from Lesson 12).

The stream processor's real job: cleaning GPS

Raw GPS is a liar in cities. Between towers the points jitter ±20 m; in a tunnel there are none for 90 seconds. The processor applies three corrections before the point hits the index:

  • Map matching — snap the point to the road network (hidden-Markov-model matchers weigh "near a road" against "consistent with the last snapped position"). Without it, a car on the ring road shows up in the river and dispatch computes river-crossing ETAs.
  • Staleness policy — a ping older than ~30 s is removed from the available set, not kept. A parked car in a tunnel that hasn't pinged in 2 minutes is not "available 200 m away" — it's a ghost. The index TTL enforces this mechanically.
  • Outlier rejection — a point implying 400 km/h (GPS jump) is dropped; trust the previous heading and speed instead.

How the rider's map stays live

The rider's app doesn't poll the database for nearby cars. It subscribes (websocket) to a per-cell update channel: "give me the available-driver set for my geohash-5 cell every 2 seconds." The publisher is a small service that diffs the Redis set per cell and pushes deltas. Fan-out math: 300K rider apps × one cell subscription each = 300K lightweight subscriptions, and the publisher only recomputes cells whose driver set actually changed between ticks. This is the Twitter-feed fan-out problem in miniature — push wins because the updates are sparse relative to the subscribers.

The tunnel problem (name it before the interviewer does): GPS doesn't work underground, and riverside metros are full of tunnels and parkades. The system must treat "no data" as a first-class state — ghost-bust with TTLs, never interpolate a position into the dispatch index, and show the rider "updating…" rather than a confident lie. Every location system has a tunnel chapter; yours should too.

05

Dispatch: turning candidates into a driver

Finding nearby drivers is a lookup; choosing one is a decision. Dispatch is the decision engine, and it must never assign one trip to two drivers.

The six-step dispatch

  1. Candidate pull. Expanding ring search (§3) returns driver ids, each with a fresh (≤30 s) location.
  2. ETA scoring. For each candidate, estimate pickup ETA from road-network distance. Calling the routing engine per candidate is too slow at rush scale, so the standard trick is a cached zone-to-zone travel-time matrix: precompute drive times between geohash-6 cell pairs nightly/continuously, and look up matrix[pickup_cell][driver_cell] in microseconds. Fall back to live routing only for the top 3 candidates.
  3. Filter. Drop drivers who accepted another offer seconds ago, who are below the rating floor, or whose vehicle class doesn't match the request (UberX vs XL).
  4. Rank. Score = α·ETA + β·(driver idle time) − γ·(detour if driver is en route to nothing)… In practice a small weighted formula plus business rules. The one-line version: minimize pickup ETA subject to fairness (don't starve any driver) and the vehicle-class constraint.
  5. Offer. Send the top driver a trip offer with a 15-second accept window. While the offer is open, that driver is soft-reserved: invisible to other dispatches but still shown as available on rider maps (maps show approximate cars, not commitments).
  6. Commit or cascade. Accept → trip commits, other candidates release. Decline/timeout → release the soft reservation and offer the next-ranked driver (the cascade). After 3–4 failed offers, widen the ring and try again — or tell the rider "no cars, try soon."

Exactly one driver per trip: the concurrency story

Two riders 50 m apart can both pull the same driver from the index in the same second. The soft-reservation must be atomic: a Lua script on Redis does if available: move driver → offered(driver, trip, expiry=15s) as one indivisible step — the exact SET NX PX + Lua pattern from the distributed-locks lesson. The offer key expires on its own, so a crashed dispatcher can never strand a driver in limbo. Exactly-once assignment = atomic soft-reserve + TTL + idempotent commit (the trip row is created once, with the rider's idempotency key from §7).

Upfront pricing: quote before you dispatch

Riders see a price before a driver is assigned. The quote = base fare + per-km × route distance + per-min × route duration, multiplied by the current surge factor for the pickup cell (§6). The route comes from the routing engine's cached cell-pair distance — the same matrix as ETA scoring. Quotes are honored even if traffic worsens (the platform eats the difference; that's a business decision, not an engineering one), which is why the quote is computed once and stored on the trip row.

06

Surge pricing: when demand eats supply

Full time at the stadium: 25,000 fans, 400 nearby drivers. No price signal and the outcome is a lottery — random lucky fans get cars, drivers have no reason to drive toward the stadium. Surge is the feedback loop that balances the market.

Definition

Surge pricing multiplies fares in a small area (a geohash/H3 cell cluster) when rider demand exceeds driver supply there. It does two things at once: rations demand ("is this trip worth 2.4×? some fans will wait or walk to the tram") and attracts supply (drivers see the heat map and head toward the stadium). The price is a control signal, not a punishment.

The formula, honestly

Per surge cell, every minute the pricing service computes:

demand  = rider requests in the last 5 min  (requests/min, smoothed)
supply  = online empty drivers in the cell  (drivers)
ratio   = demand / max(supply, 1)
surge   = clamp(ratio, 1.0, MAX)   with MAX ~ 3.0–5.0 depending on market

Three production details separate the textbook from the real thing:

  • Discrete tiers, not a continuum. Multipliers move in steps (1.2×, 1.5×, 1.8×…) — continuous multipliers confuse riders ("why is mine 2.37× and hers 2.41×?") and make the heat map unreadable.
  • Hysteresis. The multiplier doesn't fall the instant one driver arrives; it must stay below the threshold for a few minutes. Without this the price flaps 1.5× → 1.0× → 1.5× and riders game the refresh button — the same anti-flapping instinct as autoscaling cooldowns (Lesson 2).
  • Geographic smoothing. A 4.9× cell next to a 1.0× cell creates a cliff: drivers park just outside the zone and riders walk 200 m to the cheap side. Surge zones are blurred across neighbors so the gradient is drivable.
Soccer analogy

Dynamic ticket pricing for the derby

A derby sells out in an hour at face value while a midweek cup tie has empty seats. Stadiums learned this decades ago: price the derby higher (rationing — only fans who value it most buy) and use the revenue signal to open more turnstiles and add trains (supply). Surge pricing is dynamic ticket pricing running every minute, per neighborhood, except the "seats" (drivers) can physically move toward the high price.

Where surge lives in the architecture

A pricing service consumes the demand/supply streams and writes surge:{cell} = 1.5 into a low-latency store every minute. The quote path (§5) reads it as one key lookup; the driver app reads it to paint the heat map. If the pricing service dies, quotes fail closed to 1.0× (no surge) — degraded revenue is better than absurd 5× quotes from stale data. Never let a stale surge multiplier charge a rider: every multiplier carries a timestamp, and quotes reject anything older than ~5 minutes.

07

Trip lifecycle and data model

Locations are ephemeral; money is forever. The trip record is where ephemerality ends.

The state machine

Rider taps "request"
  → REQUESTED   (trip row created, idempotency key = client_req_id)
  → MATCHED     (driver accepted the offer; soft-reserve becomes a commitment)
  → ARRIVING    (driver en route to pickup; live tracking on the rider's map)
  → IN_TRIP     (driver confirmed pickup — meter running)
  → COMPLETED   (drop-off confirmed; fare finalized; payment charged)
  → CANCELLED   (from REQUESTED/MATCHED/ARRIVING; cancellation fee rules apply)

Guardrails: only one transition is legal at a time (a row-level state column + UPDATE … WHERE state = 'MATCHED' — the conditional-update idempotency pattern from the queues lesson), and payment is charged exactly once, after COMPLETED, through an idempotent charge keyed by trip id.

Sharding the trip table

1M trips/day × 365 × 5 years ≈ 1.8B rows — this table must be sharded (Lesson: database sharding). The natural shard key is trip_id (random UUID): trip lookups are point queries ("show me trip X"), and no hot-shard risk since ids are uniform. Rider history and driver history are secondary queries served by indexes on (rider_id, created_at) — which means cross-shard fan-out for "my last 20 trips" (scatter-gather, acceptable at that rate) or a separate history store. Do not shard by geohash: the stadium at full time would be a thermonuclear hot shard.

What stays out of the hot path

  • Payments — charged asynchronously after completion via a queue (money movement tolerates seconds of delay; the queues lesson's persist-before-route applies).
  • Receipts, ratings, analytics — all downstream consumers of a trip.completed event on the stream. The trip service publishes once; pub/sub fans it out (Lesson 12).
  • Driver location history — written to cheap object storage in batches for dispute resolution ("the driver never came to my pin"), never to the trip database.
08

The hard parts: full-time surges, tunnels, cancellations

The design above works on a Tuesday. Here's what breaks on matchday — and the specific defense for each.

1. The full-time flash crowd

25,000 ride requests in 10 minutes: the ring search is fine (it's just lookups), but the offer cascade isn't — 400 drivers getting pinged, declining, re-offered, in a thundering herd. Defenses: staged rollout of the map (riders see "high demand" and a queue position instead of hammering request), batch matching windows (collect requests for 10 seconds, match the batch optimally instead of first-come-first-served — this is how the cascade cost collapses), and surge doing its rationing job (§6). This is backpressure with a user-facing face.

2. Driver vanishes mid-trip (tunnel, dead battery, app killed)

The TTL ghost-bust (§4) removes them from dispatch, but a trip already IN_TRIP with a silent driver is worse. Defense: the trip service watches the heartbeat stream per active trip — no ping for 60 s on an active trip → mark trip DEGRADED, ping the rider ("your driver's signal dropped — still on the way?"), and if silence persists, offer re-dispatch without double-charging. Exactly-once billing (the idempotent charge key) is what makes re-dispatch safe.

3. The cancellation race

Rider cancels at the same instant the driver accepts. Both events hit the trip service; the state machine (§7) admits only one: whichever transition commits first wins, the other gets "trip already CANCELLED / already MATCHED" and the UI reflects the truth. Cancellation fees are computed from the state at cancel time (cancel during ARRIVING after 5 min → fee; cancel during REQUESTED → free) — the fee rule reads the state machine, not wall-clock arguments.

4. Phantom availability on the map

Riders see 8 cars on the map but get "no drivers." Cause: the map shows approximate cars (jittered positions, sampled set — showing exact driver locations is a privacy and gaming problem), while dispatch needs committable cars. Defense: the map is explicitly a vibe ("cars around you"), the quote screen carries the honest signal, and soft-reserved drivers are excluded from the map count the moment they're offered elsewhere. Never let the map promise what dispatch can't deliver.

5. Fraud: the fake-GPS driver

A driver spoofs GPS to sit "at" the stadium collecting surge fares without driving. Defenses, layered: outlier rejection on impossible speeds (§4), cross-checking pinged position against the rider's phone GPS at pickup ("both phones must agree they're together"), and trip-path plausibility (the driven polyline must roughly match the road route). Fraud is a data-consistency problem wearing a trench coat — and the rider's phone is your second source of truth.

Senior-room summary of §8: every hard part is a consistency or staleness problem in disguise — ghost drivers (stale index), cancel races (concurrent state), phantom maps (approximate reads), fake GPS (untrusted writers). The architecture's answer is always the same shape: TTLs, atomic transitions, idempotency keys, and a second source of truth.

10

Q&A with full model answers

Four questions this design reliably produces in senior rooms — each with the complete answer you'd give, not a hint.

Q1 · Core
Why can't you just store driver locations in Postgres and query "drivers within 2 km"?
They're testing whether you feel the write/read shape before reaching for tools.

Three reasons, in order of importance:

  1. The query isn't indexable. "Distance from a moving point" is a function of two columns evaluated per row — a B-tree on (lat, lng) can't prune it, so every request scans every driver: 120K distance computations per request, 55 requests/sec at peak. PostGIS (R-tree/GiST) fixes the read shape, but…
  2. The write rate kills it. 30K location updates/sec is an OLTP write load that buries a relational row store in WAL, index maintenance, and lock contention. Locations are ephemeral — last write wins, no transactions, no joins. That's a key-value workload (Redis), not a relational one.
  3. The read pattern is "nearby set," not "row by id." We need "all members of these 9 cells" thousands of times a second — a SUNION over Redis sets keyed by geohash, each an O(1)-ish key lookup. The database still exists in this architecture — it owns trips (durable, relational, exactly-once money), just not positions.

The one-liner: positions are an ephemeral spatial stream — index them in memory by cell; trips are durable relational facts — store those in the database.

Q2 · Core
Walk me through what happens, end to end, when a rider taps "request" at full time.
The classic "follow the request" question — they're grading the sequence, not the boxes.
  1. The app sends POST /trips with pickup, destination, and a client-generated idempotency key (double-tap protection).
  2. The trip service creates the trip row in state REQUESTED, computes the upfront quote (route distance from the cached zone matrix × base rates × current surge for the pickup cell), and returns the quote.
  3. Dispatch runs the expanding ring search over the Redis geohash index: ring 0, then +8 neighbors, widening until ≥5 candidates or max radius.
  4. Candidates are scored by road-network ETA (cached cell-pair matrix, live routing only for the top 3), filtered by vehicle class and rating, and ranked.
  5. The top driver gets a 15-second offer; a Lua script atomically soft-reserves them (invisible to other dispatches, TTL'd at 15 s so a crash can't strand them).
  6. Accept → trip commits to MATCHED (conditional update on expected state — concurrent cancel loses cleanly); the rider's map subscribes to the driver's live pings. Decline/timeout → cascade to the next candidate.
  7. Driver arrives (ARRIVING), confirms pickup (IN_TRIP, meter runs on the ping stream), drop-off (COMPLETED) → an idempotent charge fires once, and a trip.completed event fans out to receipts, ratings, and analytics.

Close with the failure you handle: "If the rider's phone retries the POST, the idempotency key returns the existing trip instead of creating a second one — no double dispatch, no double charge."

Q3 · Senior depth
Two riders request at the same time and your index returns the same driver for both. How do you guarantee exactly one assignment?
They want the concurrency mechanism named, not hand-waved.

The guarantee comes from making the reservation atomic and expiring, in three layers:

  1. Atomic soft-reserve via Lua: moving a driver from geohash:{cell}:available to offer:{driver_id} happens in one Redis Lua script — check-and-set, indivisible. Two dispatchers racing both run the script; exactly one sees "still available" and wins. This is the SET NX PX + Lua compare pattern from distributed locks, applied to drivers instead of critical sections.
  2. TTL on the offer (15 s): if the dispatcher crashes mid-offer, the key expires and the driver silently returns to the available set. No stuck drivers, no manual cleanup, no lease-renewal protocol needed for a 15-second window.
  3. Idempotent commit: the winning dispatch commits with UPDATE trips SET state='MATCHED' WHERE id=? AND state='REQUESTED'. Even if two commits somehow raced, the conditional update admits exactly one; the loser's trip row stays REQUESTED and cascades to its next candidate.

The trap to dodge: don't lock the driver row in the database for the offer window — a 15-second DB lock at 55 dispatches/sec is a contention cliff. The lock lives in Redis (ephemeral, TTL'd); the database only sees the final commit.

Q4 · Senior depth
It's full time, 25,000 fans request at once, and you have 400 drivers nearby. Your system doesn't melt — how?
The "what breaks at 100×" question. They want the specific defenses, in priority order.
  1. Surge rations first. The pricing loop sees demand/supply explode and raises the multiplier within a minute — a large fraction of fans decide to wait, walk, or take the tram. The cheapest scaling is demand you never have to serve.
  2. Batch the matching window. Instead of dispatching each request the instant it arrives (first-come-first-served cascade storms), collect requests for ~10 seconds and match the batch — one optimization pass instead of 5,000 racing offer cascades.
  3. The location pipeline is already decoupled. 30K pings/sec spike to ~50K; the Kafka-style stream buffers it (backpressure by design) while the Redis index keeps serving reads. Writes and reads scale independently because a stream sits between them.
  4. Degrade the map honestly. Rider apps get "high demand — you're #N in the queue" instead of a spinning "finding driver." The queue position is a cheap counter; the alternative is 25,000 phones retrying and DDoS-ing your own dispatch.
  5. Fail closed on pricing. If the surge service lags, quotes fall back to 1.0× with fresh timestamps — a lost margin beats a viral screenshot of a 9× fare from stale data.

The framing line: "I don't scale the match — I shrink the problem: price rations demand, batching collapses the cascade, the stream absorbs the write spike, and the UI tells the truth."

Practice drills (untimed)

Three drills. Work them on paper or in your head, then check the rubric — the rubric is the lesson.

Drill 1
Size the ping pipeline for a 5× matchday spike.
Given: 120K drivers online, 4-second ping interval, 100 bytes per ping. The spike: driver count holds, but every rider app also starts polling the nearby-cars endpoint every 2 s (300K apps). Compute (a) ping writes/sec and MB/s, (b) nearby-car reads/sec, (c) where the first bottleneck appears and your one fix.
Rubric — what a good answer contains
  • (a) 120,000 ÷ 4 = 30,000 writes/sec; × 100 B = 3 MB/s (~260 GB/day).
  • (b) 300,000 apps ÷ 2 s = 150,000 reads/sec on the nearby-cars path.
  • (c) The reads are the bottleneck if they hit the database — 150K QPS of spatial queries melts Postgres. The fix: serve nearby-cars from the Redis cell sets (SUNION of 9 keys, sub-millisecond), with the rider app subscribing to per-cell deltas instead of polling. The ping writes are already absorbed by the stream buffer — name that explicitly.
  • Senior bonus: note the asymmetry — reads outnumber writes 5:1 here, which is why the index (not the ingest) gets the caching budget.
Drill 2
Design the geohash boundary fix.
A rider stands 10 m from a cell boundary; the nearest driver is 15 m away but across the boundary in the neighboring cell. Your ring-0 lookup (rider's own cell only) misses them. Explain the failure precisely and give the minimal fix, with its cost.
Rubric — what a good answer contains
  • The failure: geohash prefixes are discontinuous at boundaries — adjacent points can share no prefix, so a single-cell prefix lookup is blind across the edge. (Stadium analogy: the fan is in row A, the driver in row B — different stand codes, same wall.)
  • The fix: query the rider's cell + its 8 neighbors (3×3 block) at every ring step; ring expansion then proceeds outward from that block.
  • The cost: 9 key lookups instead of 1 per ring — constant factor, negligible against the 10,000× win over a full scan. Name that it's a constant cost, not a scaling one.
  • Senior bonus: mention H3 as the alternative that makes all 6 neighbors equidistant (no corner-neighbor special case), and why you'd still start with geohash (simpler to operate, prefix containment is free).
Drill 3
Write the surge hysteresis rule.
Without hysteresis, the multiplier flaps 1.5× → 1.0× → 1.5× as single drivers enter and leave the cell, and riders learn to game the refresh button. Specify the exact rule you'd implement (thresholds, timers), and argue why each part exists.
Rubric — what a good answer contains
  • Step up fast, step down slow: raise the tier the moment the smoothed ratio crosses the upward threshold (supply must arrive now); lower it only after the ratio stays below the downward threshold for N consecutive minutes (e.g., 3–5).
  • Gap between thresholds: upward trigger at ratio ≥ 1.5, downward only at ratio ≤ 1.2 — the dead band absorbs single-driver noise without a timer alone.
  • Discrete tiers (1.0 / 1.2 / 1.5 / 1.8 / 2.2 …): no continuous multipliers, so small ratio wiggles never change the price riders see.
  • Why: flapping destroys trust (riders feel cheated) and creates perverse incentives (drivers hover at zone edges waiting for the re-surge). The rule trades a few minutes of slight overpricing for a stable, legible market.
  • Senior bonus: geographic smoothing — blur the multiplier across neighboring cells so drivers can't arbitrage a 200-meter cliff.

Cheat sheet & 60-second framing

If the lesson evaporates under pressure, keep this page.

The six decisions

  1. Index space, not rows: geohash-5 cells as Redis set keys; "nearby" = SUNION of the cell + 8 neighbors. ~10,000× cheaper than scanning drivers.
  2. Ingest via stream, not direct writes: phone → websocket → partitioned stream → processor → Redis. The stream absorbs the full-time spike and fans out to map, dispatch, fraud, warehouse.
  3. Clean GPS before indexing: map-match to roads, TTL ghosts at ~30 s, reject impossible speeds. Never interpolate into the dispatch index.
  4. Dispatch = rings + atomic reserve: expanding ring search, ETA scoring from a cached zone matrix, Lua-script soft-reserve with 15 s TTL, cascade on decline.
  5. Surge = rationing + attraction: per-cell demand/supply ratio, discrete tiers, hysteresis (down slow), geographic smoothing, fail closed to 1.0×.
  6. Trips are relational, money is idempotent: shard trips by trip_id (never by geohash — hot shard at the stadium), state machine with conditional updates, exactly-once charge keyed by trip id.

The 60-second framing (say this first, then take questions):

"A ride-hailing platform is a location-ingestion machine with a matching service attached: 120K drivers pinging every 4 seconds is 30K writes a second, and that dominates the design. I'd index drivers by geohash cell in Redis so 'nearby' is nine key lookups, not a 120K-row scan. Dispatch expands rings outward, scores by road-network ETA from a cached zone matrix, and soft-reserves the winner with an atomic Lua script and a 15-second TTL — exactly one driver per trip, no stuck reservations. Surge is a per-cell demand-over-supply ratio in discrete tiers with hysteresis, fail-closed to 1×. Trips themselves are boring relational rows sharded by trip id, with a state machine and idempotent charges — because the money path is where 'exactly once' actually matters. For the full-time flash crowd, I'd rather shrink the problem than scale the match: surge rations demand, batch the matching window, and tell riders their queue position honestly."
